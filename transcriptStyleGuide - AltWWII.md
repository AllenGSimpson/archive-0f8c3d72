## House objective

An AltWWII transcript should sound like a well-prepared narrator explaining a complicated world to an intelligent listener. It should be specific without becoming a ledger, conversational without becoming casual, and neutral without becoming bloodless.

The governing principle is:

> Explain what exists, why it exists, how it works, what it costs, and what changes because of it.

The transcript is not propaganda for or against an in-world state. It may describe success, failure, competence, cruelty, waste, or innovation, but it should ground those descriptions in observable consequences rather than moral cheering, sneering, or hindsight performance.

## Priority order

When two style preferences conflict, use this order:

1. Established AltWWII canon and factual consistency
2. The language profile derived from the user's messages
3. Clarity for a listener
4. Neutral analytical framing
5. The structural techniques in the Battle Order reference corpus
6. Elegance, humor, or dramatic flourish

## The neutral spoken voice

### Preferred stance

Use the stance of an analyst who understands that institutions act under incomplete information. Explain the internal logic of a decision even when the result is poor.

Preferred:

> The 1948 reorganization reduced the number of rifle battalions available to each corps. In return, it concentrated artillery and transport at corps level, where those assets could be reassigned between sectors.

Avoid:

> The disastrous 1948 reorganization gutted the corps so that bureaucrats could hoard its best equipment.

The preferred version may still conclude that the reorganization failed. It simply shows the exchange before judging the outcome.

### Description before judgment

Present the arrangement, purpose, and constraint before the assessment.

Use:

> The division had enough organic transport to move one regiment at a time. That was adequate for a static frontier role, but it prevented the whole division from concentrating quickly after a breakthrough.

Avoid leading with:

> The division was badly under-motorized.

### Symmetrical standards

Apply the same evidentiary standard to allies, enemies, and neutral states. Do not describe one side's plans as doctrine and the other side's as fantasies unless the evidence supports the distinction.

### Calibrated certainty

Use three levels of confidence.

- **Established:** state the fact directly.
- **Supported inference:** use “likely,” “appears,” “suggests,” or “would have.”
- **Open question:** say what is unknown and why it matters.

Examples:

> The battalion was authorized thirty-six guns.

> Surviving readiness reports suggest that no more than twenty-four were operational in March.

> It is unclear whether the third battery ever received its tractors; if it did not, the battalion could not have moved as a single formation.

Do not use “perhaps,” “possibly,” or “probably” as padding. Attach uncertainty to a specific claim.

## Spoken-prose rules

### Paragraphs are units of thought

Each paragraph should do one main job:

- define;
- locate;
- enumerate;
- explain a relationship;
- describe an action;
- state a consequence;
- qualify;
- or transition.

If a paragraph changes both scale and time, it probably needs to split.

### Sentences should survive being heard once

Keep the main subject and verb close together. Introduce one unfamiliar acronym or coined term at a time. Place qualifications after the core statement when possible.

Preferred:

> The ministry controlled production targets. The firms still managed their own plants, but they could not freely choose what to build or where to sell it.

Less suitable for narration:

> Although retaining formal responsibility for plant-level management, firms, whose allocations were established through the ministry's annual program, lacked the otherwise customary discretion to determine either the composition of production or its destination.

### Name, define, shorten

On first use, give the full term and a plain-language definition. After that, use a stable short form.

> The Reich Air Defense Inspectorate, or RLI, coordinated the regional warning and interceptor network. The inspectorate did not command every fighter unit directly.

Do not alternate among several nicknames for the same institution.

### Dates and numbers

Use exact dates when sequence matters. Use approximate periods when false precision would distract.

Readable:

> By the spring of 1953...

> On 17 June, three days after the order...

> The brigade had about 4,800 personnel on paper and closer to 3,900 present.

Avoid clustering more than three unrelated numbers in one sentence.

### Parenthetical material

Convert most parenthetical facts into a second sentence. Spoken prose does not display brackets to the listener.

Written note:

> The 14th Regiment (minus its second battalion, then in Croatia) moved east.

Transcript:

> The 14th Regiment moved east. Its second battalion was still in Croatia and did not accompany it.

## How to build spoken lists

### Announce the axis

Tell the listener how the list is ordered.

> The corps can be divided into four functional groups: headquarters, maneuver forces, fire support, and sustainment.

This creates four mental shelves before the details arrive.

### Keep categories parallel

If the first item names a formation and its role, each later item should do the same.

> The 8th Brigade held the northern crossings. The 11th Brigade protected the rail corridor. The independent cavalry group screened the open western flank.

Do not mix a formation, a commander biography, an equipment statistic, and a political event as if they were equivalent list items.

### Count first, then unpack

> The regiment had three maneuver battalions. Two were truck-mounted infantry. The third was a mixed bicycle and motorcycle battalion intended for reconnaissance and flank security.

The listener learns the size of the container before receiving its contents.

### Use an intentional order

Choose one:

- higher echelon to lower echelon;
- command to maneuver to support;
- front to rear;
- north to south;
- earliest to latest;
- most common to exceptional;
- or side A and side B under identical headings.

If the order changes, state the pivot:

> That describes the permanent organization. In the field, the attachments changed the balance.

### Interpret after every small cluster

Do not deliver a long inventory and defer all meaning to the end.

> Each company had twelve carriers, but only nine carried rifle sections. The remaining three were used by the headquarters, mortar detachment, and maintenance team. The battalion therefore could not treat every vehicle loss as an equal loss of infantry capacity.

### Separate organic, attached, and available

These are not synonyms.

- **Organic:** permanently part of the formation.
- **Attached:** placed under the formation for a mission or period.
- **Available in support:** able to assist without joining the command.
- **Nearby:** geographically close but not necessarily usable.

This distinction should be explicit whenever it affects an order of battle.

## The explanatory chain

For every major institution, formation, or weapon, attempt to complete this chain:

> origin → purpose → structure → resources → method → limitation → consequence

Not every topic needs the same amount of space at every step, but skipping several links produces shallow worldbuilding.

### Origin

What event, reform, constraint, or inherited practice created it?

### Purpose

What problem was it intended to solve?

### Structure

Who commands it, what are its main components, and how are they related?

### Resources

What personnel, equipment, infrastructure, information, and supply does it require?

### Method

How is it expected to work in ordinary conditions?

### Limitation

What prevents it from doing more, moving faster, or operating independently?

### Consequence

What does this arrangement change for the battlefield, economy, government, or ordinary person?

## Scale management

AltWWII transcripts will often move among strategic, operational, tactical, institutional, and human scales. Signal every major change.

Useful transitions:

> At the strategic level...

> For the army group, this meant...

> At battalion level, the practical effect was simpler...

> For the crew, the change was visible in three places...

> Outside the armed forces, the same policy affected...

Do not jump directly from national steel output to one squad's ammunition load without explaining the connection.

## Causal transitions

Prefer transitions that identify a relationship.

### Cause

> Because the southern line had no parallel railway, the army depended on coastal shipping.

### Consequence

> This left the inland corps with only three days of fuel once the ports closed.

### Contrast

> The northern divisions were larger on paper. In practice, the southern divisions had more transport and could concentrate faster.

### Qualification

> That was the authorized arrangement. Wartime battalions frequently replaced the third rifle company with local auxiliaries.

### Change of scale

> That explains the national plan. The next question is what it looked like inside a single district.

Avoid empty transitions such as “moving on” when a causal transition is available.

## Answering likely audience questions

Before drafting, write down the five questions a skeptical listener is most likely to ask. Common examples:

- Why did they not use the obvious alternative?
- Who actually commanded this unit?
- Was this the paper organization or the field organization?
- How was it supplied?
- Where did the personnel come from?
- What happened when the plan met resistance?
- Is this arrangement universal or limited to one year or theater?
- What evidence supports the number?

Answer each question near the passage that raises it. Do not leave all caveats for a final disclaimer.

### Use objection pivots sparingly

> At first glance, this appears to duplicate the corps artillery. The distinction was range: divisional guns supported the immediate battle, while the corps group attacked roads, headquarters, and assembly areas farther behind the front.

This is preferable to an adversarial “you might think” repeated throughout the transcript.

## Detail selection

### A detail earns inclusion if it does at least one job

- explains a decision;
- establishes scale;
- distinguishes two similar things;
- causes a later event;
- reveals an institutional value;
- constrains an action;
- or provides human texture.

Details that do none of these belong in a reference note, not the spoken script.

### Proper nouns need anchors

Pair an unfamiliar name with type, place, or function.

> The Hohenlohe Works, the region's main armored-vehicle plant...

> General District Seven, which covered the lower Danube...

### Technical detail needs operational meaning

> The radio had a nominal range of thirty kilometers. Broken terrain reduced that sharply, so brigade headquarters relied on relay trucks whenever battalions moved beyond the valley roads.

The range alone is trivia. The relay dependency is worldbuilding.

### Human detail needs institutional meaning

> Crews slept beside their vehicles during alert periods because the brigade had no dedicated barracks near the dispersal sites.

This shows readiness, infrastructure, and daily life at once.

## Recommended transcript architectures

### Short equipment or unit transcript: 5–8 minutes

Target length: 800–1,200 words.

1. Hook and scope — 60–100 words
2. Definition and parent organization — 100–150
3. Components or personnel — 200–300
4. Employment — 200–300
5. Limitation and exception — 100–180
6. Synthesis — 60–100

### Standard institutional transcript: 10–15 minutes

Target length: 1,500–2,300 words.

1. Central question and scope
2. Historical or political origin
3. Formal organization
4. Real operating practice
5. Resources and dependencies
6. One concrete example
7. Limitations, disagreements, or variations
8. Consequence and synthesis

### Long worldbuilding transcript: 20–30 minutes

Target length: 3,000–4,500 words.

1. Cold open in a consequential moment
2. Explicit date, place, and central question
3. Rewind to the divergence or institutional origin
4. Strategic situation
5. Principal actors and order of battle
6. Operating plan
7. Chronological action or implementation
8. Friction, adaptation, and turning point
9. Immediate outcome
10. Civilian, economic, political, and institutional effects
11. What later observers misunderstood
12. Qualified synthesis

The long form should not merely be a short explainer with more inventory. It requires change over time and consequences across more than one scale.

## Campaign narrative template

Use this when writing battles, coups, deployments, crises, or fictional operations.

### Cold open

Place the listener with one formation or person at a moment when the plan has become concrete. Establish location, immediate task, and danger. Do not reveal private thoughts unless the transcript has an in-world source for them.

### Rewind

State when and why the crisis began. Explain only the strategic context needed to understand the operation.

### Order of battle

Introduce forces in a stable geographic or command order. For each major formation, give:

- location;
- commander if relevant;
- strength or principal components;
- role;
- and one limitation.

### Plan

Explain the intended sequence and the assumption on which it depends.

### Execution

Use time markers and geographic anchors. Alternate action with explanation, but do not pause every thirty seconds for a miniature essay.

### Turning point

Identify the moment when the original plan became impossible, unnecessary, or substantially different.

### Outcome

Separate tactical result, operational result, and strategic consequence. They may point in different directions.

### Aftermath

Include civilian movement, occupation policy, infrastructure, replenishment, command changes, and institutional learning when relevant.

## Organization explainer template

Use this when describing a ministry, army, fleet, corps, division, industrial combine, police system, or colonial administration.

Opening:

> In [date], [organization] was the [plain definition]. Its purpose was [problem], but its authority stopped at [important boundary].

Hierarchy:

> At the top was [headquarters]. Beneath it were [count and categories].

Functional breakdown:

> The organization can be understood in four parts: [parallel categories].

Practice:

> That was the formal arrangement. Day-to-day control worked differently because [constraint].

Exception:

> The principal exception was [case], where [different arrangement] applied.

Synthesis:

> The system centralized [capability], left [responsibility] decentralized, and depended on [vulnerability or outside resource].

## Comparison template

Begin with a common problem:

> Both states needed to [mission], but they expected to do so under different conditions.

Declare criteria:

> The comparison turns on four things: manpower, mobility, fire support, and command flexibility.

Apply identical order to both sides. Then compare under a scenario:

> On a prepared frontier, A's additional artillery mattered more. During a rapid advance, B's transport and decentralized maintenance were more useful.

Conclude conditionally:

> Neither structure was universally superior. Each concentrated resources around a different prediction of the next war.

## Historical evolution template

Use comparable snapshots:

> In each period, we will look at the same five features: headquarters, maneuver strength, fire support, transport, and replacements.

At every transition, state:

1. what changed;
2. why it changed;
3. what capability was gained;
4. what capability or simplicity was lost;
5. and whether the reform survived contact with practice.

End with the persistent trend rather than the latest table.

## Original model passage

The following is an original example of the house method, not an imitation of any source:

> By 1956, the Danubian Security Division was a division in name and a territorial command in practice. Its headquarters controlled four border regiments, but only two were intended to fight as complete formations. The other two supplied guards, patrols, and replacement companies across nearly six hundred kilometers of frontier.
>
> The division can be understood in three parts. The border regiments watched the crossings and maintained local informant networks. The mobile group, built around one motorized battalion and a company of assault guns, responded to larger incursions. Behind both sat the signals and transport command, which connected isolated posts to the regional railway.
>
> That arrangement gave the division broad coverage at modest cost. It did not give the commander four maneuver regiments. Most border companies lacked the transport and heavy weapons needed to leave their districts, and pulling them away would open the very crossings they existed to guard.
>
> The practical result was a force that could detect trouble early and contain a small incident quickly. Against a coordinated attack, it depended on army reserves arriving before the mobile group exhausted its fuel and ammunition.

Why it works:

- the first paragraph corrects a likely category error;
- the second announces and follows a three-part list;
- the third distinguishes nominal from usable strength;
- the fourth converts structure into capability and limitation;
- and the tone assesses without cheering or condemning.

## Forbidden shortcuts

Do not:

- imitate Battle Order's exact phrasing, jokes, verbal habits, or persona;
- turn a table of organization into an uninterrupted inventory;
- present authorized strength as guaranteed field strength;
- describe equipment without operators, supply, or command relationships;
- use “obviously,” “simply,” or “of course” where the conclusion is contested;
- treat doctrine as a script that units always follow;
- assign motives to a population, institution, or commander without evidence;
- confuse nearby forces with attached forces;
- confuse tactical victory with strategic success;
- use present-day moral judgment as a substitute for causal explanation;
- or hide uncertainty behind confident narration.

## Drafting workflow

### Before drafting

1. State the transcript's central question in one sentence.
2. Choose the primary architecture.
3. Establish date, scope, and point of view.
4. List the five likely listener questions.
5. Build a fact sheet with sources and confidence levels.
6. Choose the axis for every spoken list.
7. Decide what the conclusion must synthesize.

### During drafting

1. Define each unfamiliar term on first use.
2. Pair each number cluster with interpretation.
3. Pair each capability with its relevant cost.
4. Signal changes of time, place, and scale.
5. Distinguish formal organization from field practice.
6. Keep causal links explicit.

### After drafting

1. Remove details that do no explanatory work.
2. Check that every unit introduced in a campaign matters later.
3. Search for loaded adjectives and replace them with evidence where possible.
4. Verify dates, names, numbers, and command relationships against canon.
5. Read the script aloud.
6. Target 145–160 words per minute.
7. Break sentences that cannot be understood on one hearing.
8. Confirm that the ending states change, continuity, and consequence.

## Final review checklist

- [ ] The opening states or dramatizes the central question.
- [ ] The date, place, institution, and echelon are clear.
- [ ] The transcript uses one primary structural architecture.
- [ ] Spoken lists announce and maintain a clear order.
- [ ] Numbers are interpreted rather than merely recited.
- [ ] Organic, attached, supporting, and nearby forces are distinguished.
- [ ] Capabilities are paired with limitations.
- [ ] Paper organization is distinguished from field practice.
- [ ] Uncertainty is attached to specific claims.
- [ ] Major changes of time, place, or scale are signaled.
- [ ] Likely listener objections are answered near the relevant passage.
- [ ] The tone remains neutral and evidence-led.
- [ ] The conclusion separates tactical, operational, strategic, and civilian effects where relevant.
- [ ] The wording is original and does not reproduce a reference author's persona.
- [ ] The script reads naturally aloud at the target pace.

## User voice integration

The user-authored reference is the set of User turns in `transcript.md`. Its language profile is recorded in `userLanguageGuide - AltWWII.md`.

For finished transcripts, use the guide's `Polished Allen` mode:

- preserve corrective contrasts, formal-versus-functional distinctions, contractions, concrete analogies, and calibrated uncertainty;
- preserve the user's tendency to bracket misleading extremes and then define a positive middle model;
- reduce dictated fillers, clause restarts, repeated hedges, and unbroken exploratory paragraphs;
- and retain the neutral, causal, and structural requirements in this guide.

The language profile governs word choice and sentence movement. The Battle Order corpus governs explanatory architecture. Neither source overrides established canon.
