# Land Armaments Reference, 1941–1985

## Purpose and authority

This is the specialized canon reference for land armaments in the alternate-World-War-II setting. It covers the principal tanks, armored carriers, infantry fighting vehicles, artillery, air-defense weapons, antitank systems, rifles, and machine guns required to keep the setting internally consistent from the 1 August 1941 technical baseline through 1985.

Military aircraft, aerospace vehicles, and orbital weapons are governed separately by [Aerospace Armaments Reference, 1941–1985](./aerospaceArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md). Warships, fleet balances, naval designations, and the individual-hull ledger are governed by [Naval Armaments Reference, 1941–1985](./navalArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md).

Within that subject, this document supersedes:

- casual equipment names used earlier in [transcript.md](./transcript.md);
- real-world comparison labels used as placeholders;
- caliber alternatives written as “X or Y”;
- approximate specification bands where this document supplies a single service value;
- the earlier description of the German StG 60 as a full-power battle rifle.

Later direct user corrections still take precedence. This edition incorporates the August 2026 detailed-development branches, under which the older reference specifications were expressly reopened. A real-world name retained here is intentional: either the relevant institution and development path still exist, or the name is independent enough to survive the divergence. Where the transcript has reopened a later system without yet selecting a replacement, this document marks the gap as open rather than preserving the displaced specification as active canon.

This is a reference rather than a manufacturing ledger. Listed weights are standard combat weights, power figures are nominal production ratings, and performance figures describe the named baseline model. Individual vehicles and ammunition lots still vary in service.

## Scope

Included:

- Germany and its principal export system;
- Britain and the Commonwealth;
- the United States;
- Japan and its imperial production sphere;
- Italy, Occitania, Spain, Hungary, and Romania;
- Argentina as a German-derived arsenal state;
- the principal land systems used in the 1985 Falklands/Malvinas campaign.

Not yet covered exhaustively:

- every truck, prime mover, engineer vehicle, and recovery subtype;
- infantry mortars below 81 mm;
- strategic and theater ballistic missiles;
- naval and aviation weapons;
- police-only weapons;
- complete production totals.

## Naming and measurement rules

1. A comparison such as “Leopard 1 generation” describes a technological relationship, not a service name.
2. German model numbers use Roman numerals for the main tank sequence and adoption-year numbers for many supporting systems. Production variants continue the Wehrmacht-style `Ausführung` letter sequence (`Ausf. A`, `Ausf. B`, and onward), not Bundeswehr-style `A1`, `A2`, or `A3` suffixes. Export localization adds a lowercase country code after the letter, as in `Ausf. D(ar)` for Argentina; it does not create an `A4E`-style designation.
3. British names retain War Office and Fighting Vehicle number conventions where their programs plausibly survive.
4. American `M` numbers diverge once neutral America misses the historical wartime procurement sequence.
5. Japanese `Type` numbers use the final two digits of the imperial year.
6. Italian, Occitan, Spanish, and Argentine names use their own languages and institutions rather than translated German labels.
7. Vehicle weights are metric tonnes.
8. Gun length is stated in calibers: `L/44` means a barrel forty-four bore diameters long.
9. Small-arms cartridges are written as bullet diameter × case length.
10. “Introduction” means formal service acceptance. A first battalion, export delivery, or general issue may follow later.

| Period | Armor and guns | Fire control and optics | Infantry systems |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1945–1949 | APCBC, early APDS, conventional steel armor | Optical telescopes and manual ranging; active infrared experimental | Iron sights, limited optical sights, stamped assault rifles |
| 1950–1959 | Mature HEAT and APDS; first universal tanks | One-axis stabilization, optical rangefinders, active infrared in elite units | Intermediate cartridges, bullpups, battle rifles, recoilless weapons |
| 1960–1969 | Two-axis stabilization, NBC systems, first-generation ATGMs | Analog ballistic computers; active infrared common; early passive low-light sights | Assault rifles standard; integral and detachable optics practical |
| 1970–1979 | APFSDS, spaced armor, early composite inserts, improved ATGMs | Laser rangefinders, image intensification, stabilized gunner sights | Standard bloc mounting systems, underbarrel launchers, night sights |
| 1980–1985 | Composite armor and 120 mm guns on newest MBTs | Digital fire control and gunner thermal sights on elite systems | Bloc-specific accessory bars; illuminated sights limited to select units |

By 1985, a first-line new tank may combine a laser rangefinder, digital ballistic computer, two-plane stabilization, and a thermal gunner sight. Independent commander thermal sights remain rare. Export and reserve tanks commonly retain passive image intensifiers and analog fire control.

# I. Germany

## Doctrine and industrial character

Maus teaches Germany that mass alone is not a doctrine. The mature postwar formula is:

> Standardized automotive families, powerful guns, excellent observation, aggressive combined-arms integration, and enough protection to survive the expected engagement.

The wartime E-series survives as an industrial philosophy rather than a set of literal production blueprints. Germany emphasizes common engines, transmissions, tracks, recovery equipment, radios, sights, and specialist chassis. Premier German units receive the best ammunition and optics; allies and export clients receive deliberately tiered packages.

### Panzer V late-war sequence

The setting sequence is alphabetic: **Ausf. A → B → C → D → E**. Ausf. D supersedes the older reference's Panther F label. It is the final mature 75 mm Panther, with the Schmalturm, 80 mm frontal hull armor, 50 mm sides, 40 mm rear armor, and a 120 mm turret-front zone.

Ausf. E is one official Ausführung with two materially different production phases. The first Baulos forces the 88 mm KwK 43 into a heavily modified Schmalturm-derived installation and produces only twenty to thirty cramped, loader-hostile vehicles. Later Baulose use the developing Panzer IX turret, sights, electrical system, and eventually powerpack components on the Panther hull. Quartermasters and crews distinguish *frühe E* from *späte E* even though the paperwork calls both Ausf. E. The late E is the Panzer IX program's field laboratory, not a second standardized tank family.

The historical Löwe work remains a `VK` development study and never receives the Panzer VII service number. **Panzer VII is Tiger II**; Panzer VIII is Maus.

### Panzer IX

Panzer IX is the production result of the 1944–1947 Einheitsprogramm rather than a literal E-50 drawing. Daimler-Benz holds design authority and system integration; MAN supplies the non-interleaved torsion-bar running gear and a major production share; Henschel shapes turret ergonomics and leads Bergepanzer IX work; Adler's component catalogue becomes the Einheitsnormen; Krupp supplies the 88 mm installation. Panzer IX is selected because it is consistently maintainable and producible, not because one prototype dominates every trial category.

German historians call Panzer IX the first tank deliberately designed as a main battle tank because its requirement abolishes the medium/heavy battlefield distinction. British historians instead call Centurion the first tank that became one. Both claims remain part of setting historiography.

The proposed 105 mm heavy **Ausf. B is deleted**. Panzer IX remains one principal 88 mm production family. Optional active-infrared commander and driver equipment appears in the late 1940s; the standardized 1953–1954 night-fighting package adds factory electrical preparation and is issued selectively without becoming a universal new Ausführung.

### Panzer X

Panzer X replaces Panzer IX in the first line because it integrates capabilities which cannot be added cleanly to IX: a selectable 18-round automatic loader, a 105 mm gun, vertical stabilization, coincidence ranging, an independent commander's observation turret, modular rear powerpack, auxiliary power unit, automatic fire suppression, and three-station active infrared. It is not Germany's first MBT; it is the first German tank designed as an integrated technological system.

### Panzer XI

Panzer XI is not a Leopard 1 under another name. It belongs to the same broad generation but descends from Germany’s uninterrupted Panzer X program. It prioritizes strategic movement, cross-country speed, first-round hit probability, maintainability, and modular protection.

Berlin restricts the best APFSDS penetrators, passive sights, armor inserts, and electronic components. Argentina’s **Ausf. D(ar)** is an export-specific package rather than a domestic German Ausf. D delivered unchanged.

### Panzer XII

Panzer XII is accepted in 1981 and equips its first operational battalion in 1982. Its 120×570 mm ammunition family includes an APFSDS service round and a multipurpose HEAT-fragmentation round. A programmable multipurpose round remains in troop trials in 1985.

The baseline **Ausf. A** has a thermal gunner sight but an image-intensified commander sight. Commander thermal equipment is confined to the **Ausf. B** development batch and is not general issue by 1985. Panzer XII is not exported in operational battalion strength before 1986.

Kätzchen is purpose-built, not a Panzer 38 emergency conversion. The production layout places driver front-left, engine and transmission front-right, a compact turret forward of the troop space, and an unobstructed rear door. It establishes the German IFV principle unusually early: armored movement, rear exit, organic automatic cannon, a radio in every vehicle, and continued mounted participation after the infantry dismounts.

Its eleven-man Gruppe consists of the Gruppenführer/vehicle commander, driver, and gunner plus eight dismounts. The eight-man element carries one MG 42 rather than the 251-era pair because the vehicle's 20 mm cannon and coaxial MG remain part of the squad's weapons establishment. A Panzergrenadierzug normally has three rifle Kätzchen, a dedicated command vehicle, and an 8 cm mortar Kätzchen; Panzerjäger Kätzchen are company assets attached downward, while 12 cm mortar variants sit at battalion.

Greif is the standard chassis for command, 120 mm mortar, artillery observation, antitank, air-defense, recovery, and export light-tank variants.

### StG 45A

StG 45A is a gas-operated, stamped-steel reliability development of StG 44. It is not identical to the historical roller-delayed StG 45(M). The roller-delayed Gerät 06 work survives separately and informs Gewehr 58 and later machine-gun engineering.

### StG 60

StG 60 is a small-caliber assault rifle, not a G3-like battle rifle. It uses a gas piston, rotating bolt, stamped receiver, 420 mm barrel, and 3.7 kg loaded service weight. Cuba licenses it as **Fusil de Asalto Modelo 60 (FA-60)**. Argentina imports German-built rifles under the service designation **Fusil Automático Argentino Modelo 67 (FAA-67)** and manufactures their 5.6×40 mm ammunition under license.

### StG 82 and mounting standard

StG 82 adds polymer furniture, ambidextrous controls, folding stock, three-round burst, an underbarrel 40×46 mm grenade launcher, and the **Einheitsleiste 79** mounting bar. The bar is a German dovetail-and-cross-slot standard, not a Picatinny rail. Upgraded StG 60A6 rifles can accept an adapter, but StG 82 is not universal by 1985.

# II. Britain and the Commonwealth

## British armored doctrine

Britain preserves the universal-tank concept, strong rifled guns, prepared-position fighting, long-range reconnaissance, and an expeditionary design discipline imposed by the Commonwealth’s geography. Its best tanks are heavy, but its reconnaissance and carrier fleets remain deliberately compact.

Charioteer is a reserve and export stopgap, not a heavy tank or a developmental ancestor of Chieftain. Caernarvon remains a training and development bridge between Centurion and Conqueror. FV4202 remains a three-vehicle test program feeding Chieftain; it is never a service tank.

Britain's wartime development tree remains deliberately broad: A13, Covenanter, Crusader, Cavalier/Centaur/Cromwell, Challenger/Avenger, Comet, and Centurion overlap with the Matilda, Valentine, Churchill, Excelsior, Ram, Australian AC, Archer, Firefly, Achilles, and AA families. This menagerie is initially a survival mechanism distributed across Britain and the Commonwealth. Centurion ends it functionally by absorbing maneuver and antitank roles into one universal tank.

Centurion Mk 1 reaches Egypt in 1945 and fights late Panther and scarce Tiger II vehicles. Mk 2 is the 1946 desert-corrected model built around observation, faster target acquisition, improved controls, and standardized modular night-fighting preparation. Mk 3 is formally accepted in 1947 with the 20-pounder and mature wartime optical and electrical package, but loses its intended theater before operational deployment. The later claim that Mk 3 was designed specifically to defeat Panzer IX is an anachronism: British procurement first sees the complete Panzer IX at the 1947 Siegestag parade, although its turret looks familiar from late Panzer V Ausf. E combat reports.

Chieftain engine reliability improves earlier than in real history because Britain treats German armor as an immediate strategic threat. Mk 8 introduces a laser rangefinder and improved automotive package. Mk 10 adds composite/applique turret protection, passive night equipment, and improved analog-digital fire control.

Challenger exists in 1984 but is not deployed to the Falklands. The fleet is small, support is concentrated in Britain and Canada, and the Army will not risk its newest sixty-two-ton tank in a difficult recovery theater.

# III. United States

## Procurement divergence

Neutral America is the war's great armored observer and prototyper rather than a combat-selected mass armored power. It has money, proving grounds, industrial breadth, attaché reports, and anxiety about foreign tank growth, but lacks battalion experience which would expose the difference between brochure performance and combat-ready tank-days. Its wartime establishment therefore overlearns visible qualities—gun, armor, weight, and maximum speed—and underlearns maintenance, recovery, fuel, and crew burden.

The M3, M4, M5, and T1/M6 programs survive because they are committed or already producing hardware at the divergence. From 1942 the Advanced Medium Tank Experimental Program buys information rather than a mass replacement fleet: T20, T22, T22E1, and T23 test transmissions, electrical drive, turret layouts, and automatic loading in one-to-three-vehicle lots. The T22E1 becomes one of America's earliest serious autoloading tank experiments, but its awkward replenishment and malfunction clearance earn the verdict “interesting; do not build more.”

The Philippine War is the institutional shock. It teaches the Army and Marines that transport weight is a combat characteristic, tracks matter more than road speed in difficult terrain, and a tank which ships, bridges, ports, recovery vehicles, and workshops cannot sustain has only theoretical protection. It vindicates M24 in that theater without proving that every future war should be fought by light tanks.

## American nomenclature

American `T` and `M` numbers do not form a simple matched ladder. Standardizing T34 as M34 does not imply that every M-number has a same-number T predecessor; M46, for example, is an M26 modernization. `E` marks an experimental modification and `A` an accepted production modification. Complete equipment nouns remain part of the designation, so reuse across different materiel categories is lawful.

The former compact M7/M9/M10/M11/M12/M13/M14 alternate tank ladder is withdrawn. It conflicted with the later authorial decision that the M24, T20, M26, M34, M41, T92, and delayed Patton-family development remain the recognizable American lineage.

### Production and factional memory

M4 production remains in the low thousands and is principally a Commonwealth export; the earlier exact 8,000/5,200 split is reopened pending the naval and industrial production audit. Britain modifies part of the fleet into Firefly and related vehicles and repeatedly reports that the original gun and protection are inadequate against late-war threats.

The 1947 Army contains three institutional schools. Cavalry receives M24; the medium school receives a small M26 fleet and retains T20-family hardware; the heavy school operates one T34 battalion which becomes M34 Marshall after Maus appears at the Siegestag parade. T32 fails because it reaches heavy-tank weight without M34 firepower. T43 develops an M103-like Maus killer, but Germany's actual adoption of the 53-ton Panzer IX destroys its production case.

In the Philippines, M24 earns the headlines and T20 vehicles perform competently. The heavy school correctly notes that the campaign does not test its continental mission, then incorrectly tries to dismiss every lesson about shipping, bridges, fuel, recovery, and workshop burden. The postwar result is four factions: heavy M34/T43 advocates, Pershing-modernization officers, an expeditionary-medium school, and cavalry purists. M41 becomes the vehicle each claims; the cavalry purists answer its weight growth with the amphibious T92E1.

The delayed Patton-family sequence follows from that argument. M46 is chiefly a powerpack modernization of the small M26 fleet. M47 combines proven automotive work with the best lighter-medium turret and fire-control studies. M48 is the first clean-sheet post-Philippine general-purpose medium. M60 becomes the mature 105 mm family after real hemispheric interventions finally give America operational feedback. Exact post-1968 M60 modification dates and the 1980s successor competition remain open; the displaced M11A3/M13/XM14 claims must not be used to fill that gap.

## American armored doctrine in 1985

Armored divisions center on the mature M60 family; M5 battle carriers; M2 scouts; M4A2 air-defense guns; M8 self-propelled artillery; missile carriers; helicopters; and strong recovery units. A replacement competition is active, but its winner and service designation remain open.

Marines combine M3 Cougar, light tracked direct-fire detachments, amphibious M4 carriers, helicopters, and naval fire support. Exact 1980s light-tank service designations remain open.

The doctrine is summarized:

> Do not carry sixty tons where twenty will accomplish the mission. Do not send twenty tons where only forty can survive it.

# IV. Japan

## Designation and doctrine

Japan retains the imperial-year service-type system. The number records the final two digits of the imperial year in which a system is standardized; it is not a project sequence:

| Gregorian year | Imperial year | Service type |
|---:|---:|---|
| 1944 | 2604 | Type 4 |
| 1947 | 2607 | Type 7 |
| 1956 | 2616 | Type 16 |
| 1964 | 2624 | Type 24 |
| 1966 | 2626 | Type 26 |
| 1976 | 2636 | Type 36 |
| 1982 | 2642 | Type 42 |
| 1985 | 2645 | Type 45 |

For armored vehicles, the public registry form is:

> Type year + family prefix–Iroha ordinal + optional modification term

Thus **Type 16 Shu-Ha** and **Type 16 Automatic Rifle** are different registry items. A descriptive class such as “main battle tank” or “armored personnel carrier” may follow in technical prose, but it does not replace the family–ordinal code in the formal name. The full imperial year may appear in procurement instruments, but normal military usage shortens Imperial Year 2616 Type Shu-Ha to Type 16 Shu-Ha.

The second syllable follows the Iroha ordering—`I`, `Ro`, `Ha`, `Ni`, `Ho`, `He`, `To`, `Chi`, `Ri`, `Nu`, `Ru`, `Wo`, `Wa`, `Ka`, `Yo`, and onward—and canceled projects consume positions. It is the design ordinal within a functional family, not a Type year or a `Kai` modernization mark. A production vehicle therefore need not be the first member of its family.

### 1952 Imperial Armored Materiel Designation Standard

Japan regularizes new armored and specialized-vehicle projects in 1952. Wartime names remain valid even where their categories were inconsistent, but new projects use the following functional prefixes:

| Medium tank | Chi | チ | Established wartime medium-tank family |

| Heavy tank | Ju | ジュ | `jū sensha`, heavy tank |

`Shiki` (式, Type or system) and `Gō` (号, number or model) are not vehicle-family prefixes. `Ka` identifies amphibious design rather than mere service with naval infantry. The hyphen joins a family prefix to its Iroha ordinal; it does not separate the Type year from the code. Ordinary cargo trucks normally receive a Type year and descriptive capacity—such as Type 22 Three-Ton Truck—without an Iroha armored-family code.

The reform is an imperial-management measure as much as a linguistic one. Korean and Manchurian production, transfers to Nanjing China and associated states, Army–Navy duplication, competing zaibatsu, and the arrival of missile and radar vehicles make the wartime mixture of office shorthand and vehicle nicknames untenable.

Japanese design prioritizes shipping and rail movement, narrow roads and bridges, jungle and mountain performance, excellent observation, compact diesels, high power-to-weight ratios, and mechanical reliability. Japan accepts lighter side protection than Germany and becomes an early leader in hydropneumatic suspension and compact fire-control systems.

# V. Italy and the Latin bloc

## Latin ammunition policy

The Latin bloc standardizes selectively rather than erasing national industries.

- **7×51 mm LC** becomes the full-power rifle and machine-gun cartridge in 1958.
- **5.6×45 mm LC** becomes the small-caliber assault-rifle cartridge in 1968.
- **25×137 mm** and **30×170 mm** are the principal light-vehicle cannon cartridges.
- **105×617R mm** is the shared 1960s tank cartridge.
- **120×560R mm LC** is the new 1980s tank cartridge.
- The **Guida Comune 79** is the Latin optical/accessory mounting bar. It is not Picatinny.

Italy initially retains 7.35×51 mm, while Spain retains 7×57 mm. Both remain in reserve stocks after standardization.

## Occitania

### Industrial origin

The **Toulouse Evacuation of 1946–1947** moves selected AMX, APX, SOMUA, and artillery engineers, drawings, tools, and prototypes south before France’s final partition. The successor organization is:

> **Ateliers Mécaniques d’Occitanie (AMO)**

AMO claims the technical inheritance of French armored design without claiming administrative continuity with German-controlled Paris.

Spain does not receive the German engineers who historically created CETME; they remain inside victorious Germany. Spain instead develops gas-operated rifles, machine guns, wheeled vehicles, artillery, ammunition, and licensed Italian/Occitan armor.

## Hungary and Romania

Hungary fields a small production run of 44M Tas in 1947 while Turán and Zrínyi remain active. By 1956, German economies of scale end independent Hungarian main-tank production. Hungary concentrates on licensed Panzer components, artillery, recovery vehicles, ammunition, and local modifications.

Romania follows a similar path and specializes in petroleum-service vehicles, mountain equipment, ammunition, and German-licensed systems. Northern France uses German equipment almost entirely.

# VI. Argentina

## Industrial character

Argentina imports complete German weapon systems while manufacturing compatible ammunition, ordinary spares, radios, and support equipment. The major exception is the domestic TAM/VCTP family: Argentine-designed vehicles built with German technical assistance and imported German engines, transmissions, guns, optics, electronics, and other high-value components. Spanish-language documentation, local modifications, and a national South Atlantic doctrine make the resulting force operationally Argentine without implying licensed domestic manufacture of German weapons.

Panzer X Ausf. D(ar) equips reserve, training, and mainland defense units by 1985.

Argentina purchases fifty-four Panzer XI Ausf. D(ar) tanks. Ten deploy to the Falklands as the elite mobile reserve around Stanley and Mount Pleasant. Ausf. D(ar) has a laser rangefinder, two-axis stabilization, passive image intensification, an export spaced-armor package, and German-compatible 105×617R mm ammunition. It does not have domestic German Ausf. D armor inserts or the Reich’s best APFSDS.

TAM-74 is an Argentine design begun in 1970 with German technical assistance and imported German high-value components. It draws on the automotive and layout experience of Schützenpanzer 71 Greif without being a licensed Greif derivative. It has MBT-class firepower but only cannon-fragment, rifle, and limited frontal autocannon protection. A2 adds laser ranging, improved stabilization, and passive night sighting.

VCTP-74 is the domestically designed infantry carrier of the TAM program. It shares the Argentine hull and automotive architecture while using imported German power-train, turret, gun, optics, and electronic components.

The **Vehículo Blindado de Exploración Modelo 78 (VBE-78 Ñandú)** is a German-built six-wheeled reconnaissance vehicle imported under an Argentine service name. Its German suspension, transmission, turret, and optics use Argentine-licensed 30×170 mm ammunition. It is amphibious in calm water, carries four scouts behind its three-person crew, and serves as the fast reconnaissance complement to the tracked TAM/VCTP family.

# VII. The 1985 Falklands/Malvinas land battle

## Canon matchup

The old **Challenger-versus-Panzer XII** shorthand is retired. Neither side deploys its newest parade-ground tank.

The principal armored fight around Mount Pleasant pits mature 1960s and 1970s systems upgraded for the early 1980s:

- British Chieftain Mk 10 against Argentine TAM-74A2 and Panzer XI Ausf. D(ar);
- British Cavalier, FV432, and CVR(T) against Argentine VCTP-74;
- Swingfire against Argentine armored reserves;
- Dragón and Panzerfaust 74A against British armor.

## Remaining decisions

This edition intentionally leaves only matters that require later narrative or visual-development work:

- unit names and commanders in the 1985 Falklands/Malvinas campaign;
- exact production totals beyond the fixed Argentine Panzer XI purchase;
- visual silhouettes, factory marks, camouflage standards, and detailed ammunition nomenclature;
- complete post-1991 replacement programs.
