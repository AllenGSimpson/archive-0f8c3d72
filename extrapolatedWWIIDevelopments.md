# Extrapolated Second World War Developments, 1941–1947

## Purpose and authority

This document closes the high-leverage gaps identified in the final wartime-development turns of the [compiled transcript](./transcript.md). The compiled transcript is the controlling source. Earlier setting-bible, timeline, wiki, and armaments-reference statements remain useful only where they are compatible with it. When this document supplies a missing date, order of battle, institutional decision, or force estimate, that answer is an extrapolated working-canon lock rather than a claim that the transcript had already stated it.

The status language is intentionally explicit:

- **Established** means that the author fixed the outcome or the transcript repeatedly relied upon it.
- **Locked extrapolation** means that this document selects one compatible answer under the author's permission to complete missing material. It should be treated as working canon unless later authorial correction supersedes it.
- **Writer discretion** means that local detail may be invented inside stated guardrails without another canon consultation.
- **Open** is reserved for details whose selection would add little autonomy but could unnecessarily constrain later work.

This is a completion dossier, not a replacement transcript. It supplies five things the final transcript turn requested: an authoritative wartime chronology, a campaign-outcome matrix, a 1947 military snapshot, a supersession and errata sheet, and rules for writer discretion.

## Controlling causal model

The war is not won by one German wonder weapon or lost through universal Allied incompetence. Several competent institutions face cumulative constraints which their individual successes cannot remove.

Germany converts the failed quick victory of 1941 into a methodical eastern war. It improves winterization, rail conversion, recovery, siege doctrine, tactical aviation, and standardization while accepting that fortified Soviet centers must often be isolated rather than stormed. The Soviet Union survives far beyond the point at which strategic reversal remains plausible because it trades offensive reach for defensive depth, evacuates industry, simplifies production, and delegates military authority. Those adaptations preserve resistance but also create the local commands which outlive the state.

Italy wins the Mediterranean war because Malta falls, its supply system becomes reliable rather than limitless, its armed forces learn from repeated combat, and British commitments multiply. Japan wins a limited imperial war because it avoids attacking the United States, takes a short southern perimeter, concentrates on China, and turns north only after Soviet collapse has made intervention comparatively safe. Britain remains tactically dangerous throughout. It loses because every theater, convoy, repair, aircraft replacement, and escort commitment consumes a depth which no belligerent America replenishes.

The decisive sequence is therefore:

> **Malta → failed Torch → prolonged Egyptian and Sinai war → collapse of the Soviet strategic centers → Ural armistice and Japanese northern intervention → loss of Suez and Sinai → American aid cutoff and late Atlantic crisis → Second Battle of Britain → White Parachutes → Westminster Crisis → Attlee peace ministry → Lisbon.**

## Operation Torch and the Mediterranean endgame

### The Torch expedition

The expedition is intentionally large enough to be a national commitment and too small to reproduce the historical Anglo-American operation. The figure “112,000 ashore” is not the initial assault strength. It is the peak administrative strength after reinforcements, air-ground personnel, port troops, and replacements have accumulated.

| Component | Approximate peak strength | Function |
|---|---:|---|
| British 78th Infantry Division and attached forces | 23,000 | Principal Algiers assault and eastern field force |
| British 6th Armoured Division and army tank elements | 17,000 | Mobile reserve; mixed Crusader III, Churchill, early Cromwell detachments, and supporting artillery |
| 1st Canadian Division group | 15,000 | Oran and western security; later reinforces the central perimeter |
| Australian–New Zealand composite division group | 14,000 | Built around experienced brigade groups rather than stripping both dominions of complete home-defense armies |
| South African brigade/divisional group | 10,000 | Motorized infantry, reconnaissance, artillery, engineers, and logistics |
| British commandos, airborne, antiaircraft, engineers, signals, port, medical, and RAF ground establishment | 33,000 | Landing, airfield, port, supply, and theater functions |
| **Peak ashore** | **about 112,000** | Approximately 74,000 arrive in the first assault lift |

The Commonwealth composition is political as well as operational. Britain supplies the majority because London will not ask the dominions to carry another Gallipoli. Canadian, Australian, New Zealand, and South African participation is substantial enough that defeat becomes a Commonwealth trauma. American citizens serve as volunteers in British or Commonwealth formations and air units; there is no American national contingent.

Alexander recognizes the compromised operation within days. His priorities are Algiers and Oran, defensive depth, and a possible Atlantic-Moroccan supply alternative. His caution does not save Torch because the problem is throughput rather than tactical awareness. British ASW reinforcement reduces U-boat freedom, but every escort assigned to convoys weakens the fleet screen. The Battle of Cape Bougaroun proves that the Royal Navy can still win a surface engagement; it also leaves the Mediterranean supply problem intact.

The campaign lasts sixty-six days. It is long enough for the Franco-Italian coalition to demonstrate real competence and short enough that the bridgehead never becomes a self-sustaining North African state. Vichy formations neither collapse nor transfer en masse. The P40's first provisional battalion fights unevenly: the vehicle is useful, its crews are green, and Rommel's greatest contribution is recovery, reserve discipline, and coalition staff method rather than magical maneuver.

### Egypt, the Canal, and Sinai

The fall of Cairo and the loss of Suez are separate events. This distinction removes the principal contradiction among the older summaries.

Italy controls most of Egypt after October 1945, but the British Canal Zone remains an armed bridge between Sinai, Palestine, and the Red Sea. Britain can contest crossings, operate from east-bank positions, and launch limited counterattacks. Centurion Mk I sees combat in the 1945 retreat; Mk II becomes a real squadron-level combat tank in 1946. Burma teaches reliability and environmental sealing, while Egypt teaches gunnery, optics, dust control, first-shot doctrine, night fighting, recovery, and the cost of fighting late Panthers and small numbers of Tiger II.

The final 1947 offensive succeeds because the strategic balance has changed, not because Centurion fails. German equipment and cadres are available after the eastern armistice, Italian logistics can concentrate on one connected theater, the Royal Navy cannot restore the eastern Mediterranean supply system, and American aid is ending. British withdrawals preserve formations for Palestine, the Gulf, and the postwar empire. This is one reason the Army does not acquire a second Algiers surrender myth.

## Soviet collapse and the northern war

### The fall of Stalin and the authority to negotiate

Stalin falls after Moscow, Stalingrad, and Leningrad have ceased to function as mutually supporting strategic centers. Removing him earlier would make the collective leadership appear to abandon a recoverable war. Removing him after the armistice would leave nobody able to authorize the armistice. January 1946 is therefore the hinge.

The conspiracy is an emergency coalition, not a liberal revolution. Malenkov supplies party administration, Molotov state continuity and diplomatic form, Beria control over security channels, Zhukov military credibility, and Khrushchev the public argument that continued resistance under Stalin has become national suicide. The collective State Defence Committee authorizes negotiations; Zhukov and designated front commanders authenticate military disengagement. This distributes responsibility so that no single figure can be accused of personally surrendering Russia.

The February Sverdlovsk instrument and June Perm settlement are deliberately separate. The first stops the organized war and makes evacuation possible. The second recognizes the territorial fact after lines, rolling stock, population movements, and successor claims have become clearer. German transfers begin after February, but Berlin retains enormous forces east of the former front because conquest has created an occupation and anti-partisan problem rather than eliminating one.

### Evacuation and Red Army fragmentation

Industrial evacuation begins in 1942 and never becomes one clean movement. By 1945 the surviving rail system prioritizes machine tools, engine plants, design bureaux, trained workers, archives, communications equipment, and selected formations. The protected 1946 corridor saves a meaningful state and military remnant; it does not empty European Russia.

Organized armies retreat east in three categories:

1. formations still connected to the collective high command move under the armistice schedule;
2. isolated armies negotiate local passage or dissolve into successor commands;
3. security, partisan, and ideological formations reject the agreement and continue irregular war.

Battlefield appointment and retrospective commissioning have already given local commanders control over troops, depots, and districts. The post-Soviet warlords therefore emerge from a rational emergency personnel system rather than appearing without ancestry after June 1946.

| Baltic | Becomes part of Fortress Leningrad, then is partly scuttled and partly inherited after December 1945. Japan's northern intervention follows Soviet military defeat and the Ural armistice process; it does not cause Germany's victory. Its first campaign is limited to the Maritime Province, Amur rail and river systems, Sakhalin-related security, and bargaining with eastern commanders. This preserves the difference between the German continental conquest and the Japanese construction of associated Far Eastern states.

## The British peace sequence

The exact political chain is now locked:

> **White Parachutes → London and Westminster demonstrations → Cabinet and coalition crisis → confidence defeat → Churchill resigns → Attlee Emergency Ministry → Lisbon preliminary ceasefire → Lisbon settlement → White Parachute Election → Bevan Reconstruction Ministry.**

Attlee is the emergency prime minister. He is credible because no 1945 election occurred and Labour has remained inside the wartime coalition. He does not announce surrender. He asks whether acceptable terms exist while the armed forces remain at readiness. The Chiefs of Staff do not publicly intervene in politics, but their assessment—that further resistance cannot restore the Soviet front, American support, Suez, the escort balance, or secure southern airspace—makes the parliamentary break defensible.

Churchill could constitutionally negotiate. He cannot politically transform seven years of promised reversal into an armistice without destroying the coalition's last shared language. His resignation makes peace governable and preserves him as the embodiment of resistance rather than the signatory of defeat. George VI appoints the person who can command the Commons, refuses personal government, and refuses to make the Army the instrument of preserving one ministry against peaceful crowds.

The 8 June ceasefire is the practical end of Anglo-Axis combat. The 18 July Lisbon Instruments are the settlement. This separation permits fleet notifications, local standfast orders, prisoner accounting, shipping lanes, and the difficult treatment of distant forces without pretending that every gun on every frontier stops at the instant a conference begins.

## Air development outside Germany and Britain

The German and British paths are already the best-developed air branches in the transcript. The controlling late-war facts remain: the Luftwaffe uses a small number of long-lived families rather than an indiscriminate Luft '46 catalogue; the He 162 remains paper or experimental; the Do 335 does not become a principal service type; Me 262, Ar 234, Ho 229, the HG-III branch, and the Me 364 Höhenbomber have distinct roles; and a mature piston night-defense system defeats Britain's deep bomber offensive before the Second Battle of Britain. Britain retains Meteor, Vampire, Attacker, Canberra, and Hawker-derived development as overlapping institutional answers constrained by engines. The Canberra begins as a hurried Derwent-powered bomber rather than appearing with a mature Avon by miracle. Britain demonstrates carrier jet trials but has no operational wartime carrier-jet arm.

The four less-developed powers require the following locks.

### Italy: from elegant airframes to a service system

Italy's 1941 problem is not an absence of good designers. It is the inability to combine advanced engines, radios, armament, production, spares, and desert support at German or British scale. The C.202 proves that a modern Italian fighter around a licensed German engine can be excellent. Malta, Egypt, and Torch then force Italy to treat reliability and production as combat characteristics rather than factory matters.

| Period | Engines and manufacturers | Types and decisions | Combat lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1941–42 | Alfa Romeo and Fiat radials remain important; licensed Daimler-Benz DB 601 production supports the Macchi C.202. Macchi, Fiat, Reggiane, Savoia-Marchetti, and CANT remain distinct design centers. | C.200 and G.50 shift to secondary and fighter-bomber work; C.202 becomes the premier fighter; SM.79 and CANT Z.1007 remain useful maritime and medium-bomber families. | Desert filters, radios, cannon armament, maintenance access, and fuel-system protection matter more than nominal top speed. |
| 1943 | Fiat's RA.1050 licensed DB 605 becomes the desired common high-performance piston engine. | C.205V enters quickly as the low-disruption bridge. G.55 is selected as the principal new production fighter. Re.2005 is retained in small high-performance and evaluation batches rather than becoming a third equal mass-production ecosystem. | Torch shows that technically good aircraft lose strategic exchanges when replacements, radar control, and field repair are inferior. |
| 1944–45 | Fiat receives the largest production burden; Macchi concentrates on iterative fighter and reconnaissance work; Reggiane and Caproni form the reaction-fighter team. German-built Jumo 004s precede an imperfect Fiat/Isotta-Fraschini localization effort. | G.55 derivatives and C.205s form the piston core. The P.108 heavy-bomber effort is curtailed after limited maritime/specialist service; Italy does not build an uneconomic strategic-bomber fleet. The joint **Reggiane Re.2007** prototype flies in late 1943 and a small service group becomes operational in 1944. | Italy becomes the third power to operate a true turbojet combat aircraft, but dozens of jets supplement rather than replace hundreds of piston fighters. |
| 1946–47 | German turbine metallurgy and Italian production practice gradually improve engine life. | Re.2007 groups defend northern Italy, escort selected maritime strikes, and appear over the late Canal campaign. The Re.2007 designation is a locked extrapolation for the first operational Caproni–Reggiane type. Campini-Caproni supplies the reaction-propulsion research office, test practice, ducts, and political ancestry; Reggiane supplies the fighter airframe team; Germany supplies the first true turbine engines and manufacturing assistance. The Campini N.1 remains historically important but mechanically a dead end.

The Fiat G.55, Macchi C.205, Reggiane Re.2005, SM.79, and CANT Z.1007 therefore survive for clear reasons. The Fiat G.56 and other advanced piston proposals become trials or limited batches rather than automatic production. Large Italian strategic-bomber and excessive three-fighter-family procurement are the principal cancellations.

### Japan: southern range, Chinese endurance, northern ruggedness

Japanese development remains divided between Army and Navy establishments throughout the war. Cooperation improves at the level of engines, materials, and late jet work, but Japan does not obtain a unified postwar-style air force in 1943. The separate `A`, `B`, `D`, `J`, `G`, and `Ki-` cultures remain institutionally real.

Japan completes the transition from A6M2 to later Zero marks without pretending that the airframe remains dominant indefinitely. The N1K, Ki-84, and Ki-100 are the most important late piston survivors. The G4M remains an influential long-range family but receives protection and fire-suppression improvements at the cost of range. Projects whose historical urgency depended upon American incendiary raids or anticipated invasion are reduced to research.

### Soviet Union: progressive narrowing

The Soviet air industry does not forget aerodynamics. It loses the ability to keep every engine, alloy, factory, fuel, and mission branch healthy simultaneously. The correct trajectory is narrowing, dispersal, substitution, and cannibalization—not a return to biplanes as the official ideal.

| Period | Engines and manufacturers | Types and decisions | Combat lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1941–42 | Klimov VK-105 production anchors Yak and Pe-2 families; Shvetsov M/ASh-82 supports the radial alternative; Mikulin AM-38 supports Il-2. LaGG's structure is retained long enough to accept the ASh-82 and become La-5. | Survival requires types which can be produced eastward and repaired with imperfect labor, fuel, and materials. |

The Yak-9, La-5/7, Il-2, and Pe-2 survive because they map onto existing engines and production systems. MiG-3 mass production, strategic-heavy-bomber expansion, and competing advanced fighter families are sacrificed. This is compatible with limited local technical surprises: an isolated regiment can still be dangerous without implying Soviet theater-wide air parity.

### United States: a powerful laboratory without a combat production curve

The United States is not disarmed. Rearmament appropriations, the Two-Ocean Navy Act, existing export orders, and fear of a dangerous world all predate the divergence. What disappears is the political and operational machinery which historically demanded aircraft by the tens of thousands and revised them through continuous American combat.

| 1941–42 | Allison, Packard, Pratt & Whitney, Wright, and General Electric retain enormous capacity. The surviving American types are those funded before the divergence or justified by export, continental, and naval requirements. Enormous B-29 fleets, thousands of escorts and transports, wholesale amphibious-air specialization, and historically huge production blocks do not appear. Attachés, American volunteers, the RAF, China, and commercial missions create a real but incomplete combat-learning system.

### Jet-entry order

Because “first” depends upon definition, historians continue to dispute Britain and Germany. The useful operational ordering is:

1. Germany conducts the first military-effect turbojet sorties.
2. Britain and Germany form formal operational jet units close enough together for the squadron-first claim to remain disputed.
3. Italy fields the Re.2007 in small operational numbers in 1944.
4. Japan fields the Kikka in late 1945.
5. The United States forms a limited P-80 service group in September 1946.
6. The Soviet Union does not field an operational turbojet unit before state collapse.

## The Atlantic war and the Royal Navy

### Development of the duel

The late Atlantic war is not a reversal in which British sailors forget ASW. It is a worsening exchange ratio between two sophisticated systems.

| Period | German system | British/Commonwealth response | Result |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1941–42 | Type VII/IX wolfpacks, B-Dienst routing intelligence, surface night attack | Convoys, ASDIC, HF/DF, radar, corvettes, destroyers, patrol aircraft | Britain learns to break contact chains but lacks American belligerent depth. |
| 1943 | T5 acoustic torpedoes and escort-first attacks | Foxer, support groups, centimetric radar, Hedgehog, improved HF/DF | Escorts become deliberate targets; old U-boats still suffer heavily. |
| 1944 | Schnorchel, inshore submerged patrols, Alberich experiments | Squid and improved depth-finding sonar, coastal air patrol, sonobuoy trials, escort carriers | The contest moves from finding surfaced boats to detecting small masts, wakes, and quiet submerged contacts. |
| 1945 | First useful Type XXI flotillas and distributed ambush groups | Fast outer support groups, River/Loch-class frigates, long-range aircraft, better plotting | Convoys usually survive, but escorts and trained crews are consumed. |
| 1946 | Type XXI becomes routine; Type XXVI is the elite escort hunter; operational code security is rebuilt | Quieter escorts, decoys, ahead-thrown weapons, air-surface prosecution | Britain can kill advanced boats but cannot protect every route at tolerable cost. |
| 1947 | Secure German operational traffic, escort-first doctrine, mixed XXI/XXVI and older-boat ambushes | Concentration on essential convoys, abandonment or delay of secondary sailings, preservation policy | Merchant arrivals continue while repair backlog and route scarcity become politically decisive. |

Type XXI is the general ocean attack submarine. Type XXVI is the high-performance Walter-powered hunter assigned to the hardest screens; its peroxide system remains secret well after the war. Type XXV is the extremely fast teardrop experimental branch. Its low-30-knot submerged sprint is battery-limited, its recharge cycle is severe, and it does not enter mass production. Its classified hydrodynamic and control work reappears in the later Type XXX nuclear lineage rather than in the public diesel-electric sequence.

Escort losses are strategically larger than the sinking column. A damaged destroyer occupies a berth, machinery, skilled workers, spares, and a replacement crew. Older ships remain afloat after their effective combat careers have ended. By 1947 a nominal escort total therefore conceals ships in refit, awaiting refit, cannibalized, relegated to training, or restricted to coastal work.

The Flower class is remembered as the emergency survivor of the first half of the war. River and Loch frigates become the late Atlantic “hero” classes because they combine endurance, sonar, ahead-thrown weapons, and tolerable construction cost. Black Swan sloops and Hunt escorts remain valued specialists. Battle-class destroyers are the fast fleet and outer-screen answer, not economical substitutes for every convoy frigate.

## Japanese and American naval audit

### Japan

Japan's carrier force does not suffer Midway, but that does not produce unlimited carriers. Ceylon validates the combination of carrier reach and battle-line protection. The enlarged resource empire simultaneously demands tankers, freighters, escorts, repair ships, patrol craft, and convoy aircraft. These claims restrain the fantasy of turning every available hull into a carrier.

**Yamato** remains the active prestige flagship and is repaired after Ceylon. **Musashi** completes as a battleship. **Shinano** also completes as a Yamato-class battleship in 1945 because the Midway conversion trigger never occurs. Her completion is slow and expensive, but cancelling a far-advanced super-battleship after Ceylon would contradict the battle's institutional lesson.

**Taihō** proceeds because it is already a purpose-built armored fleet-carrier program. The **Unryū** design proceeds as the more economical wartime carrier, but only three hulls reach service by the ceasefire. There is no emergency mass conversion equivalent to the historical late-war program and no suicide-carrier doctrine. Older fleet carriers survive at much higher rates than in real history, although operational losses, accidents, and the long Indian Ocean/China war remove some aircraft and one older light carrier.

At the 1947 settlement Japan has thirteen fleet/light carriers afloat, ten operational: *Akagi, Kaga, Sōryū, Hiryū, Shōkaku, Zuikaku, Taihō,* three *Unryū*-class ships, and three light or converted carriers in varying readiness. Training ship *Hōshō* is not counted as a modern operational carrier. Japan has eleven capital ships afloat, nine operational, including all three Yamato-class ships. These figures are substantial without importing a Pacific war against the United States which never occurred.

Japanese submarine doctrine remains fleet-oriented longer than Germany's. Large I-boats scout, attack warships, carry aircraft or special detachments, and raid long imperial routes. Ceylon and the Indian Ocean gradually make commerce interdiction more respectable, but there is no American trans-Pacific logistics system to teach Japan the historical target set. Northern operations add minelaying, reconnaissance, coast watching, and support for remote garrisons. Japan ends 1947 with roughly eighty ocean and fleet submarines afloat and about fifty-eight operational; incompatible Soviet prizes are not absorbed into this count.

### United States

American naval construction starts from a large inherited authorization. It then slows under neutral budgets instead of accelerating through combat replacement. The result is a balanced fleet selected by argument, exercises, and foreign observation rather than by Pearl Harbor, Coral Sea, Midway, Guadalcanal, and the submarine campaign.

Two **Montana-class** battleships—*Montana* and *Ohio*—commission in 1946–47. The remaining three authorized hulls are suspended or cancelled before material commitment becomes irreversible. All four Iowa-class ships complete more slowly than in real history. The older battle line remains because no Pearl Harbor attack removes it, although the least useful ships enter reserve. The two Alaska-class large cruisers complete as scouts and fast heavy escorts.

The Essex program survives because *Essex* was laid down before the divergence and naval aviation remains obviously important. Five Essex-class ships are complete by the end of 1947, not the historical wartime flood. They join *Enterprise, Yorktown, Hornet, Lexington, Saratoga, Ranger,* and *Wasp* or their surviving equivalents, producing twelve fleet/light carriers afloat and nine operational after normal refits. The absence of Pacific combat preserves hulls but also produces less experienced deck organizations and no vast wartime replenishment train.

The 31 December 1947 neutral American fleet contains approximately:

| Category | Afloat | Operational | Principal limitation |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Capital ships, including Alaska-class | 20 | 14 | Large reserve and training burden; several old ships unsuitable for high-speed carrier work |
| Fleet/light carriers | 12 | 9 | Powerful but operationally untested against a peer |
| Cruisers | 38 | 29 | Fewer wartime emergency hulls than the old reference assumed |
| Destroyers | 96 | 72 | No requirement to escort a global two-ocean belligerent system |
| Major ocean escorts | 44 | 30 | The largest visible gap from historical American mobilization |
| Submarines | 86 | 62 | Excellent fleet boats, but torpedo and doctrine defects receive less brutal correction |

The United States does not build historical masses of escort carriers, destroyer escorts, LSTs, Liberty ships, or fleet oilers. That makes the fleet formidable near American bases and less capable of sustaining an immediate transoceanic offensive. Submarine torpedo defects and overly rigid prewar assumptions persist longer because there is no combat scandal to force rapid correction. The postwar American rearmament problem is therefore not discovering how to build ships; it is learning how to operate a balanced peacetime fleet against powers whose institutions have six years of combat adaptation.

## Military position at the end of 1947

Exact division and squadron counts are inappropriate where the transcript has not built a full mobilization ledger. The following is the controlling capability snapshot.

### Germany

Germany has the most experienced continental combined-arms system, but a large portion of the army is tied to occupation, forced-labor security, rail protection, anti-partisan war, and the new Ural frontier. Panzergrenadier formations are the expensive laboratory: organic communications, mounted support weapons, assault rifles, recovery, maintenance, and increasingly specialized platoon assets. Ordinary infantry adopts selected ownership principles without becoming uniformly mechanized.

The armored force contains mature Panzer V marks, Tiger families, assault guns, and the first Panzer IX/Einheit vehicles. The Panzer V sequence is A–B–C–D–E; D is the final 75 mm Panther, while E is the troubled 88 mm stopgap and Panzer IX technology demonstrator. Panzer IX appears at the victory parade but is not yet the universal tank of every division.

The Luftwaffe possesses the strongest operational jet arm and the deepest mature night-defense network. It is not infinitely ready: Me 262, Ar 234, Ho 229, HG-III, and Me 364 formations are specialized, expensive, maintenance-intensive fleets. The surface navy remains secondary to Dönitz's submarine arm. Type XXI is operationally mature, Type XXVI elite and scarce, and Type XXV experimental.

### Italy and the Latin-Mediterranean system

Italy ends the war as the dominant Mediterranean naval and logistical power. Its army has genuine experience in amphibious war, desert sustainment, coalition command, and prolonged mechanized fighting. P40 and later domestic armor remain less abundant than German equipment; semoventi, artillery, captured equipment exploitation, and maintenance discipline are central strengths.

The Regia Aeronautica has a strong G.55/C.205 piston establishment and small Re.2007 jet groups. The Regia Marina has *Aquila* operational from 1944, *Sparviero* operational as the secondary light/escort carrier from 1946, a surviving modern battle line minus *Giulio Cesare*, and growing escort/amphibious infrastructure. Italy controls Malta, the central Mediterranean, Egypt, and Suez, but must garrison a vast and politically difficult imperial corridor.

### Japan

Japan possesses the strongest carrier-battle-line combination in the Indian and western Pacific oceans, extensive land-based naval aviation, and a victorious but institutionally divided Army/Navy air system. It has not fought the United States and therefore lacks experience against an American industrial fleet. China, jungle bases, convoy protection, and the northern campaign have stretched manpower and shipping even without a Midway catastrophe.

The Army remains predominantly light and unmounted rather than copying Panzergrenadier mechanization. It has improved artillery, antitank, cold-weather, and self-loading-rifle programs, but rail, horse, river, truck, and foot mobility remain normal. Kikka jets are scarce; Ki-84/Ki-100 and later Zero/N1K families carry most air combat. The Pacific Fleet agreement gives Japan control over a useful Russian naval establishment without making Soviet ships standard IJN units.

### Britain and Commonwealth

Britain is defeated, not disarmed. The Army retains experienced formations in the home islands, Palestine, the Gulf, India, Ceylon, East Africa, and the wider empire. Centurion Mk III is accepted as the next standard tank just as the Egyptian combat theater disappears. The RAF retains an integrated national defense system, but its Meteor and Vampire force is exhausted, piston types have returned to front-line interception, and runway/engine/crew reserves are thin.

The Royal Navy remains the single institution capable of denying an invasion of the British Isles and sustaining a reduced empire. Its problem is readiness and geography, not the absence of hulls or knowledge. The postwar force will favor merchant shipping, frigates, ASW aviation, repair capacity, fleet submarines, minesweepers, and light/escort carriers around the Cape route.

### Soviet remnants

There is no unified Red Army in the western sense after June 1946. Successor governments inherit formations, airfields, factories, institutes, and military districts in unequal combinations. Many units still call themselves Soviet. Others obey a general, republic, city, railway authority, or emergency committee. Modern equipment survives in pockets while ammunition, engines, fuel, and trained maintenance become decisive local currencies.

### United States

The United States has the world's greatest latent industrial and scientific capacity and no six-year combat institution. Its Army, Navy, and Air Corps are large by peacetime standards, modern in selected equipment, and strategically inexperienced. It possesses no wartime atomic weapon. Its balanced fleet, limited jet groups, observers, volunteers, and export-derived lessons provide a foundation for rapid later rearmament without granting it historical 1945 force size or doctrine.

## Secondary-theater guardrails

### Finland and the Arctic

Writers may create Finnish, German, Soviet, and local Russian actions around Karelia, Lake Ladoga, Murmansk, the Kola railways, White Sea approaches, Arkhangelsk communications, and the later Arctic frontier. They may decide tactical winners, commanders below the level of national leadership, individual ship fates not named here, and local surrender arrangements.

They may not:

- turn Finland into the conqueror of Leningrad by itself;
- eliminate the German role in the eastern victory;
- prevent Finland from receiving its established Greater-Finnish and Arctic custodial sphere;
- create an intact sovereign USSR on the Arctic coast after the Ural settlement;
- transfer the whole Baltic or Northern Fleet to Finland without accounting for scuttling, damage, German claims, and local successor forces.

### Balkans, Greece, and the Adriatic

Writers may develop anti-partisan campaigns, Italian-German command rivalry, Croatian, Serbian, Greek, Montenegrin, Slovenian, Hungarian, Romanian, and Bulgarian local operations, and the performance of minor fleets and air units. They may invent battles whose results alter local security or careers.

They may not preserve Yugoslavia as a postwar sovereign state, remove Italy's Adriatic/Aegean primacy, prevent Hungary and Romania from receiving their established settlement outcomes, or create a partisan victory which expels the Axis before Lisbon. Tactical partisan successes are compatible; a strategic liberation is not.

### Vichy France and French North Africa

The Armée d'Afrique is a competent co-victor in Torch and remains under French officers. Individual collaborationists and genuine resistance networks both exist. Writers may invent unit actions, intelligence failures, political disputes, and French-Italian friction.

They may not convert Vichy en masse to Britain during Torch, reduce French forces to passive scenery, make Rommel commander of the entire French army, or erase the shared veteran memory that later supports an Italian-aligned Occitania. Postwar northern, Breton, Occitan, and underground French claims remain distinct and must follow their dedicated political references.

### Burma, Thailand, and the Indian frontier

The older account in which Rangoon and Burma collapse historically is superseded. Thailand is the principal attacking state. Britain and the Raj regain the defensible Burmese position by 1946. Japan plans but does not execute a major campaign to reverse that result before the ceasefire.

Writers may develop the Thai order of battle, Burmese politics, Indian units, jungle operations, local airfields, and the final 1946 push. They may not give Japan a completed conquest of Burma at Lisbon or erase the fact that Britain's local victory fails to reverse the general war.

### Partisan and occupation wars

Partisans can win ambushes, temporarily liberate districts, destroy infrastructure, rescue prisoners, assassinate officials, and force large occupation commitments. These successes should matter. They do not, before 1947, overthrow the German continental empire, restore Poland or Yugoslavia, or recreate a unified Soviet west. German, Italian, Japanese, Vichy, and client forces may be tactically defeated without changing the locked settlement.

### Minor fleets

Writers may assign individual destroyers, submarines, minesweepers, auxiliaries, river craft, and captured ships where no named disposition is fixed. They must preserve the category outcomes:

- Baltic fleet partly scuttled and partly inherited;
- Black Sea Fleet mostly destroyed;
- Pacific Fleet institutionally preserved under Japanese supervision;
- Italy loses *Giulio Cesare* at Torch;
- Britain loses *Warspite* at Ceylon and *Eagle* at Malta;
- *Hood, Prince of Wales,* and *Repulse* cannot reappear after their established losses;
- *Shinano* is a battleship, not a carrier;
- American emergency wartime carrier and escort totals do not silently return.

## Writer-discretion rules

Writers should use a three-question test before inventing wartime material.

1. **Does it change a locked strategic outcome, border, succession, or institutional lineage?** If yes, it requires authorial review.
2. **Does it only explain how a locked outcome occurred locally?** If yes, it is normally writer discretion.
3. **Would the invention silently settle a named open problem outside 1941–47?** If yes, leave it open or flag it rather than using the wartime article to decide it accidentally.

Writers may freely create, where compatible:

- battalion, squadron, ship, and local formation actions;
- junior and mid-level original characters;
- tactical victories by the strategically losing side;
- equipment faults, field modifications, repair practices, crew experiences, and local nicknames;
- propaganda slogans and memoir disputes, provided they do not replace established terminology;
- local surrender, evacuation, prisoner, resistance, and civilian episodes;
- exact minor-unit composition within the strength and capability envelopes above.

Writers must not independently change:

- the dates and political chain of Torch, Soviet armistice, Suez, White Parachutes, confidence defeat, ceasefire, and Lisbon;
- which side holds the major theaters at settlement;
- the non-entry of the United States;
- the absence of wartime nuclear weapons;
- the fate of the named capital ships and the role of *Shinano*;
- the order in which Italy, Japan, America, and the Soviet remnants enter the jet age;
- the distinction between a capable British military and an unsustainable British strategic position;
- the survival of the Crown, Parliament, Royal Navy, and an armed British state;
- the post-Soviet fragmentation which the Red Army's own emergency decentralization helps create.

The individual who first authorizes or substitutes the White Parachute load remains intentionally unnamed. Writers may show several aircrew, ordnance, and local-command decisions contributing to the event, but should not canonize a single famous originator without authorial review.

The wartime document does not resolve unrelated later questions such as Mussolini's exact death instrument, mature computing nomenclature, Raumhafen architecture, or other postwar political and technical points still tracked in [Questions and Confirmations](./questionsAndConfirmations%20-%20AltWWII.md). Writers should not infer answers to those issues from a convenient wartime sentence.
