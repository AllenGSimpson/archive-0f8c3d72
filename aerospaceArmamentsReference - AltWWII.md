# Aerospace Armaments Reference, 1944–1985

## Purpose and authority

This is the specialized canon reference for military aviation, aerospace vehicles, orbital combat craft, and their principal weapons in the alternate-World-War-II setting. It records the designation systems, representative operational lineages, service doctrines, and fixed planning specifications required to keep the setting consistent through 1985. Programs already authorized for service from 1986 through 1988 are recorded as committed development rather than a complete post-1985 inventory.

Land systems are governed by [Land Armaments Reference, 1944–1985](./landArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md). Warships, embarked naval capacity, fleet balances, and the individual-hull ledger are governed by [Naval Armaments Reference, 1944–1985](./navalArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md).

Within that subject, this document supersedes:

- casual aircraft names used earlier in [transcript.md](./transcript.md);
- real-world comparison labels used as though they were in-setting designations;
- speculative game aircraft treated as historical prototypes;
- approximate performance bands where this document supplies one reference value;
- the earlier assumption that America creates a historical independent United States Air Force;
- the earlier proposal for a mature American B-52-centered strategic force;
- any description of orbital combat as aerodynamic dogfighting.

Later direct user corrections still take precedence. A historical name retained here is intentional: the responsible institution, manufacturer, requirement, and design path survive the divergence well enough for the designation to remain useful.

This is an operational reference rather than a complete production ledger. Performance figures describe a standard service aircraft in clean or stated combat configuration under normal conditions. Actual range, ceiling, speed, payload, and endurance vary with weather, fuel, stores, maintenance, altitude, and mission profile.

## Scope

Included:

- Germany and its postwar Reich Aircraft Register;
- Japan's Army, Navy, joint-service, and guided-weapon systems;
- the United States Army Air Corps, Naval Air Corps, and Aerospace Force;
- Britain, Canada, Italy, Occitania, Spain, and Portugal;
- principal operational fighters, interceptors, strike aircraft, bombers, patrol aircraft, and close-support aircraft;
- American orbital-strike vehicles, German Raumjäger, Japanese supervised orbital interceptors, and orbital recovery doctrine;
- the major aerospace milestones needed to interpret the force structure of 1985.

Not covered exhaustively:

- every trainer, liaison aircraft, transport mark, helicopter, engine, radar, or missile subtype;
- civil airliners except where they directly affect military technology;
- complete production totals or squadron locations;
- the full launch-vehicle and lunar-transport catalog;
- naval shipboard antiaircraft systems already outside the land reference;
- post-1988 aerospace fleets.

## Naming and measurement rules

1. A phrase such as “Starfighter-type” or “Valkyrie-generation” describes ancestry or physical resemblance, not an in-setting service designation unless a formal name is listed here.
2. Introduction means formal service acceptance. A prototype flight, trial detachment, general issue, or export delivery may occur earlier or later.
3. Maximum speed is given in kilometers per hour at the altitude where the aircraft attains it. Mach numbers are descriptive and rounded to one decimal place.
4. Radius means combat radius with the listed standard mission load. Ferry range is stated separately when relevant.
5. Service ceiling is the normal operational ceiling, not an absolute zoom-climb record.
6. Aircraft weapon calibers use bore diameter × case length where the cartridge is standardized in the setting.
7. German production variants use letters and subvariant numbers; prototypes use `V`, preproduction aircraft use `-0`, and conversion packages use `/R` or `/U`.
8. Japanese formal Type numbers use the final two digits of the imperial standardization year. Army `Ki-` and Navy letter-number-manufacturer codes remain technical project identities.
9. American aircraft retain a principal role letter even when capable of secondary missions. `F/A` is not an accepted service category.
10. Orbital velocity, relative velocity, delta-v, cross-range, and endurance are stated separately; an orbital craft has no meaningful aircraft-style top speed.

## Common technological clock

| Period | Airframes and propulsion | Sensors and controls | Weapons and doctrine |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1944–1949 | First turbojets, mixed piston-jet fleets, swept-wing research, rocket interceptors | Optical sights, early airborne radar, primitive ejection seats | Cannon, unguided rockets, radio-command glide weapons |
| 1950–1959 | Reliable turbojets, afterburners, swept wings, transonic and Mach 2 flight | Radar-ranging sights, analog intercept computers, inertial navigation | First practical infrared and radar-guided air-to-air missiles |
| 1960–1969 | Turbofans, titanium structures, Mach 2–3 aircraft, rocketplanes | Pulse radar, terrain following, digital research controls, data links | Beyond-visual-range missiles, anti-radar weapons, guided anti-ship strike |
| 1970–1979 | Operational spaceplanes, variable geometry, mature VTOL, early fly-by-wire | Pulse-Doppler radar, look-down trials, integrated electronic warfare | All-aspect missile development, precision conventional strike, orbital gun trials |
| 1980–1985 | Digital fly-by-wire, composites, efficient turbofans, reusable military orbiters | Look-down/shoot-down radar, digital navigation, thermal and optical orbital tracking | Networked missiles, guided reentry weapons, operational orbital interceptors |

By 1985, the best atmospheric combat aircraft combine pulse-Doppler radar, inertial navigation, digital weapons computers, integrated electronic countermeasures, and reliable beyond-visual-range missiles. These capabilities remain concentrated in first-line German, Japanese, American, British, Canadian, Italian, and Occitan formations.

# I. Germany

## Designation system

The German system is governed by the **Reichsluftgerätordnung 1948**, the Reich Air Materiel Regulation of 1948. It cleans the central RLM register without replacing the manufacturer-number tradition.

1. Existing aircraft retain established designations.
2. The public type remains manufacturer abbreviation plus centrally assigned number.
3. Internal studies retain `P.` or `EF` numbers but cannot enter service under them.
4. Clean-sheet postwar operational types begin in the 500 block.
5. `V` identifies a prototype, as in `Me 262V12`.
6. `-0` identifies preproduction aircraft, as in `Ta 183A-0`.
7. A major production form uses a letter.
8. A subvariant uses a number after the letter, as in `Me 501A-1`.
9. `/R` and `/U` identify field and factory conversion packages.
10. Mission class is an administrative entry rather than part of the painted type designation.

The Army uses a sequential `Ki-` project number, a formal Type-year service designation, and a public name. The manufacturer is not encoded in the Ki number.

The **1956 Imperial Joint Aircraft Register** standardizes the public form as:

> Type year + Role+Iteration+Manufacturer+Variant + name

The **1968 Unified Air Procurement Regulation** makes the expanded Navy-pattern code normal for new joint-service combat aircraft. Army `Ki-` numbers survive for prototypes, helicopters, liaison aircraft, and projects developed solely by Army bureaus. The new public form was then retroactively applied to extant designs still in service.

# III. United States

## Three air services

The United States does not create a historical all-purpose independent Air Force.

| Service | Institutional position | Principal mission |
|---|---|---|
| United States Army Air Corps | Organic branch of the Army | Battlefield air superiority, attack, close support, reconnaissance, transport, and helicopters |
| United States Naval Air Corps | Organic branch of the Navy | Fleet defense, carrier strike, patrol, antisubmarine warfare, airborne warning, and naval reconnaissance |
| United States Aerospace Force | Independent strategic service from 1962 | Strategic strike, continental interception, long-range reconnaissance, missiles, spaceplanes, and orbital warfare |

Before the Philippine War, strategic bombers and experimental rockets answer to a small joint board. The Philippine experience produces **United States Aerospace Command** in 1958. Kennedy elevates it into the **United States Aerospace Force** in 1962.

The controlling principle is:

> The Army owns the air above the battlefield. The Navy owns the air above the fleet. The Aerospace Force owns everything whose natural horizon is another continent—or the edge of space.

## Designation system

Through 1957 the Army retains `P` for pursuit alongside `A`, `B`, `C`, `R`, `T`, and `X`. Aerospace Command changes active pursuit types from `P` to `F` in 1958 without resetting their numbers. The Navy retains its manufacturer-role system through 1962.

The **Joint Aircraft Designation Act of 1963** places future Army, Navy, Marine, and Aerospace Force aircraft in common role sequences:

| Prefix | Principal role |
|---|---|
| F | Fighter or interceptor |
| A | Attack or close support |
| B | Strategic or theater bomber |
| P | Maritime patrol |
| R | Reconnaissance |
| C | Transport |
| E | Special electronic mission |
| T | Trainer |
| H | Helicopter |
| X | Experimental aircraft |
| OV | Reusable orbital vehicle |
| OSV | Orbital strategic vehicle |

Aircraft retain a principal role even when they can carry secondary stores. `F/A` is rejected as bureaucratically evasive and doctrinally corrosive.

## The Maracaibo Shock and atmospheric retreat

In 1966, German-designed Venezuelan radar and surface-to-air missiles ambush an American B-58 strike near Maracaibo. Two bombers and one suppression interceptor are lost. The system wins by predicting the route and placing an intercept box ahead of the formation rather than chasing it.

The event becomes the **Maracaibo Shock**, or **the Day the Ceiling Closed**. It establishes that no atmospheric combination of speed and altitude remains permanently safe.

American responses proceed in three stages:

1. **1966–1974:** electronic warfare, anti-radar missiles, decoys, terrain following, and stand-off weapons improve atmospheric penetration.
2. **Project Apex, 1967–1969:** boost-glide and upper-atmosphere strike tests investigate routes above conventional air defenses.
3. **Project Zenith, authorized 1977:** military spaceplanes test orbital inspection, rescue, controlled deorbit, and conventional strategic attack.

By 1985, atmospheric bombers remain useful for signaling, theater wars, electronic escort, and targets poorly aligned with orbital access. New strategic investment increasingly goes to orbit.

# VI. Orbital military systems

## Legal settlement

The **Geneva Convention on Mass-Effect Weapons in Outer Space, 1974** is a limited anti-WMD proposal, not a demilitarization treaty.

It prohibits biological, chemical, and radiological-dispersal weapons in orbit and devices intended to contaminate orbital space or a celestial body on a mass scale. It does not prohibit conventional high explosive, guided kinetic penetrators, antisatellite weapons, armed inspection craft, conventional cannon, reconnaissance, or peaceful nuclear power sources.

Britain, neutrals, most South American states, Argentina, and the Latin bloc sign. America signs with an interpretive declaration protecting conventional kinetic and counterspace systems. Germany accepts registration and rescue provisions but refuses intrusive weapons inspection. Japan delays ratification because it believes the agreement freezes German-American advantage. The convention therefore has moral weight without controlling every military space power.

The **Stockholm Orbital Safety and Salvage Convention, 1976** is broadly accepted because all powers benefit. It establishes:

- registration of large orbital objects;
- breakup notification and shared conjunction warnings;
- protected distress frequencies and rescue beacons;
- liability for long-lived debris;
- passivation and disposal plans;
- protection for marked rescue and clearance tugs;
- prohibition of intentional fragmentation tests in heavily used orbital bands;
- prohibition on ejecting cases, links, covers, bolts, or loose equipment;
- emergency authority to stabilize or tow another state's disabled spacecraft.

## Debris-safe combat doctrine

American and German analysts describe collision cascades between 1968 and 1970. A 1971 debris impact and a 1972 upper-stage breakup make the danger public. The accelerated 1973 collision-cascade model gives English-language planners the term **Kessler effect**; German doctrine uses `Trümmerkaskade`.

The preferred gun engagement is **same-direction, ahead-of-track interception**. The interceptor phases ahead of a prograde target, turns its weapon retrograde, and fires backward toward the approaching craft. Missed projectiles lose orbital energy and enter the atmosphere.

Every orbital fire-control system enforces a safe-fire cone. Prograde and cross-track fire require specific authorization. Ammunition uses captive links and retained cases. The purpose is to disable sensors, radiators, communications, docking systems, or propulsion rather than shatter the target.

Gunfire does not normally deorbit a stable spacecraft. Recovery craft must rescue personnel, stabilize tumbling vehicles, safe weapons and energy systems, quarantine salvage, attach deorbit packages, and catalog remaining fragments.

The mature force-wide ratio is three support, rescue, or clearance craft for every dedicated interceptor. This is an organizational ratio, not a formation of three tugs accompanying each fighter.

## German Raumverteidigung

Germany concludes that an American orbital bomber must be intercepted in orbit. Its Raumjäger have no wings, aerodynamic tail, runway capability, or natural forward direction. They are launched by rocket, serviced at fixed depots, and remain in space.

The Orbitaler Raumhafen's standard reference orbit is a circular 720 km, 5-degree-inclination orbit supplied from the Kribi equatorial complex. Its operational defense belt extends beyond that orbit, but 720 km is the fixed altitude used for planning figures and comparative calculations.

## Japanese optical-control system

Japan enters orbital defense later and rejects both the American reusable crewed bomber and German crewed harbor fighter. Its rule is:

> The machine flies the trajectory; the human commands the engagement.

Uncrewed interceptors receive high-level orbital commands through a narrow-beam laser relay mesh. Local computers handle burn timing, phasing, collision avoidance, station keeping, fuel optimization, and sensor tracking. Human pilots aboard the command facility retain terminal rate control; a weapons officer must identify the target and close the firing circuit.

If the optical link fails, the interceptor safes its weapons, ceases closing, moves outside collision range, attempts another relay, and returns to a holding orbit. It never interprets silence as permission to fire.
