United States

The United States is a late-rising continental power whose neutrality during the general war spares it destruction but denies it victory culture, mass mobilization, and the presumption of global leadership. Its postwar politics turn on how to keep rival empires out of the Americas while deciding what kind of republic should carry that mission.

Neutrality and its consequences

Japan does not attack Pearl Harbor, and the United States never enters the general war. It receives no historical wartime cure for the Depression, no mass veteran generation, no comparable aviation and industrial explosion, no Manhattan Project, and no national memory of rescuing the world.

The resulting intervention debate never closes. Victory, long-war, fortress, limited-intervention, and mission-nationalist schools disagree over whether entry would have liberated Europe, prolonged the conflict into the 1950s, or militarized the Republic without producing victory. The Philippine War—not Europe—becomes the principal American soldier myth.

Americans nevertheless grow up under the shadow of victorious empires. German rockets, Japanese carriers, British defeat, and the unresolved Philippines make foreign power visible without producing one simple “free world” narrative. The transcript's American social sketches emphasize cynicism: every great power speaks of freedom while guarding an empire.

The restored gold dollar

Private convertibility remains suspended through the Depression and long war, but the United States never builds a universal Bretton Woods dollar system. The Byrnes administration begins a resumption debate; Thomas Dewey completes a staged Gold Dollar Restoration around 1949–51 at a working $35 per fine troy ounce.

The convertible dollar becomes the most widely accepted hard reserve claim in fragmented markets without becoming the world's single currency. Its confidence advantage is constrained by redemption: persistent foreign deficits can still force tighter credit, spending restraint, controls, devaluation, or suspension.

The American Hemisphere

Foreign policy begins as a defensive Monroe system and becomes the named American Hemisphere: an exclusionary order rather than one uniform treaty alliance. Washington fights to exclude Einheitspakt, GEACPS, and Latin Bloc strategic penetration. Interventions in Honduras and Venezuela teach that continental scale does not eliminate local resistance or technical surprise.

The 1968 Mid-Atlantic Ridge Accord draws a rough boundary between German Europe and the American Hemisphere. It prevents direct deployments across the line but leaves commerce, intelligence, companies, volunteers, and clients as loopholes. Canada discovers that American geographic claims may be as dangerous to an ally as to a rival.

The American mission state

Kennedy's Hemispheric Renewal Act joins domestic modernization to a renewed Monroe Doctrine. The independent United States Aerospace Force, the Honduran Security Crisis, and the Venezuelan intervention make aerospace capacity, technical education, industrial depth, and hemispheric command parts of one national project. The Maracaibo Shock shows that this machinery can still be surprised and defeated locally.

The civilian face of the mission is the American Orbital Vehicle Program. From Canaveral Aerospace Field, the runway-launched Columbia system reaches the Moon in 1971; Orbital Port Columbia becomes a continuously occupied repair and logistics center in 1979, while Kennedy Lunar Station extends permanent habitation beyond Earth. Project Pilgrim and Liberty Yard then turn the same reusable transport network toward Mars.

The disputed Establishment Compact of 1968 turns mission politics into a legitimacy crisis. Buchanan later channels it through the National Mission and Productive Enterprise Act, Project Zenith, and the American Productive Commonwealth Act. Yet the Rockwell Commission, failed National Unity Amendment, and leaked Narrative Shaping memorandum expose the constitutional, judicial, and propagandistic limits of government by national mission.

National Renewal

National Renewal becomes a durable fascist-adjacent mass party rather than a fringe Nazi organization. It combines hemispheric mission, corporatism, labor discipline, youth formation, racial politics, anti-bank populism, industrial modernization, and selectively American constitutional imagery.

Charles Lindbergh is its early national figure. His Minnesota victory in the 1952 election and role in forcing the 1956 contingent election make the party constitutionally unavoidable. George Lincoln Rockwell learns disciplined electoral rhetoric and wins a popular plurality in the 1968 election, but the House selects Nelson Rockefeller through an establishment compact. The resulting “Stolen Mandate” becomes the party's foundational grievance. Rockwell's 1975 assassination produces a martyr; Pat Buchanan prevents retaliatory violence and turns the movement toward lawful victory in 1976.

The Buchanan era, 1977–1985

Buchanan wins the presidency at thirty-eight under the formula “Peace, production, and ascent.” He governs through visible national missions rather than immediate constitutional revolution. Industrial credits, machine tools, semiconductors, technical institutes, spaceplanes, orbital construction, and domestic procurement tie cultural nationalism to material production.

The party never becomes ideologically uniform. Buchananites, mission technocrats, Christian-social members, congressional institutionalists, radical corporatists, and a Rockwell old guard disagree over how far victory should alter the Constitution. Buchanan kills the 1981 National Unity amendment with the declaration that the movement was given the Republic to restore, not replace.

America avoids historical deindustrialization because China is not an open low-wage platform, rival blocs restrict foreign control, and strategic production remains domestic. The system still generates favoritism and failed automation schemes; the 1982 productivity recession forces consolidation and stricter competence rules.

Personal computing

Personal computing becomes the least planned and most culturally important success of the industrial program. Federal procurement, school purchases, semiconductor research, small-business deductions, telecommunications, user clubs, and hobbyist culture create a mass market for software, spreadsheets, word processing, games, and home programming.

Elias Rowan Mercer treats the Concord computer as a coherent civic object, but his controlled formats and political National Renewal sympathies make critics call it a “gated republic.” Martin Avery Keene's Bridge environment pursues compatibility and licensed portability instead. Their rivalry turns architecture, interfaces, publishing, and foreign licensing into public political questions.

Culture and society

American culture develops without wartime victory or a universal veteran generation. The Spectator War makes radio and competing interpretation unusually important; the 1957 Rocket Awakening makes technical vocation the language of delayed modernity; and the Conspiracy Republic turns constitutional legitimacy, assassination, and institutional truth into the defining anxieties of the 1970s.

Popular music remains institutionally plural. Buddy Holly, Ritchie Valens, and J. P. Richardson survive 1959, while the Motown cultural system anchors a wider Black American public sphere. Stronger household continuity and personal computing also support an American cottage-industry revival.

Reagan and constitutional realignment

Ronald Reagan loses heavily to Buchanan in the 1980 election but rebuilds the Democratic-Republican opposition around patriotic constitutionalism and competitive enterprise. In the 1984 election he captures much of National Renewal's language of Providence, family, frontier, production, and national greatness while arguing that the republic itself—not the governing party—is the mission.

Reagan's victory and Buchanan's peaceful certification partially heal the wound of 1968. The new administration opens ordinary trade through reciprocity while protecting weapons, nuclear systems, orbital launch, ports, communications, intelligence computing, and emergency industrial capacity. Détente becomes commercial without becoming globalized or strategically trusting. The 1988 election rewards that settlement at the height of the World Market Boom.

Boom, depression, and party fracture

The 1985 Open Markets Act and 1987 Commerce Act produce the real prosperity and concealed foreign control of the World Market Boom. The ownership-mapping project exposes the aggregation failure; the National Industrial Integrity Act orders rapid divestiture; and the Great Sell-Off begins the Open Door Depression. Reagan's lame-duck recovery statute liberalizes domestic corporate reconstruction while Congress later repeals the individual federal income tax over his veto.

Thomas Harwood wins the 1992 presidential election, then breaks Renewal into five national forces through the 1993 schism after attempting compulsory industrial organization. The Omaha Rules supply the organizational routes for Constitutional Renewal, American Producers, and National Vanguard. Proportional elector rules and corporate patronage, legalized through the 1998 Public Trust Act, make later presidential contests semi-parliamentary in practice.

The 1996 election supplies no electoral majority. The House selects Andrew Prescott Hale, whose bounded stability administration normalizes a new constitutional culture: the president selects a four-year national direction, Congress repeatedly alters the method, and corporate systems preserve execution. These three clocks make the republic more continuous than its fragmented elections suggest.
