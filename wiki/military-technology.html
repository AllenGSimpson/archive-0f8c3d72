Military Technology Portal

Military modernity develops without nuclear weapons becoming the organizing fact of strategy. Armor, submarines, guided conventional weapons, sensors, aircraft, logistics, computing, and orbital systems remain usable instruments of great-power competition.

The Japanese and American military designation systems show how this complexity is administered: registry names encode acceptance dates, roles, manufacturers, service authority, and the boundary between a modification and a new procurement program.

A conventional technology clock

Jet engines, radar, guided weapons, night vision, digital fire control, nuclear propulsion, satellites, data links, and automation mature unevenly across blocs. No wartime atomic bomb creates a nuclear deterrence template, so conventional force remains politically thinkable and technologically prestigious.

Exact designation, weight, date, inventory, and status claims must follow the armament references. The setting distinguishes historical inheritance, setting extrapolation, working design, prototype, production, operational service, reserve, and retirement.

Land warfare

Germany leads modern armor, assault-rifle doctrine, night fighting, guided antitank weapons, and integrated mechanized formations. Its postwar small-arms system develops from the StG 44 family through the StG 60 and limited-issue StG 82. The Drachen and Panzerfaust system, 15 cm divisional artillery, and Flakpanzer lineage extend guided firepower and radar control below the strategic level.

The Kätzchen family establishes Germany's postwar infantry-fighting-vehicle principle; the Schützenpanzer 71 Greif becomes the 1972 standard. Panzer XI supplies the mobility-generation tank and Panzer XII the 1981 composite-armored successor.

The War of the Chiefs challenges Panzergrenadier universalism without ending Germany's mechanized core. The Congo vehicle crisis, revived Jäger arm, network-isolation method, and reforms to recovery and forward maintenance create a permanent fragmented-terrain branch.

Japan adapts high technology to ships, rail limits, weak bridges, mountains, and jungle roads, emphasizing compact vehicles, hydropneumatic suspension, and manpower-saving automation. The Type 7 Chi-Ru is its standardized victory-era tank. Its later Shu main-force lineage passes from the Type 16 Shu-Ha through the hydropneumatic Type 26 Shu-He and electronically mature Type 36 Shu-Ri to the autoloading Type 45 Shu-Ru, which begins low-rate production in 1985.

Japan's light-armor and infantry-carrier system separates the legacy Type 7 Ho-To, tracked Type 18 Yu-Ha, first-line cannon-and-missile Type 33 Ho-He, airborne Type 35 Ke-Yo, and wheeled Type 40 Yu-Ni by tactical role and cost. Its infantry-weapons sequence moves from the 7.7 mm wartime system through the widely licensed Type 24 Automatic Rifle to the 5.8 mm Type 42 Rifle, with belt-fed weapons preserving automatic support at each caliber transition.

Britain preserves heavy armor, reconnaissance families, local Commonwealth arsenals, and expeditionary repair. America divides battle taxis from reconnaissance and fields the M11A3 as its main 1985 tank while the M13 Advanced Mobile Battle Tank enters service and the XM14 Light Combat Tank enters production trials. Occitania pioneers division-level integration; Argentina combines German imports with indigenous TAM and VCTP designs.

Program histories trace American tank development from the wartime M3 through the M14 program and the Japanese Shu sequence across four competitive development cycles.

The Soviet military-industrial diaspora makes India the principal heir to several Russian design traditions. Congress fields the standardized Vijaya family, Hindustan develops more ambitious compact armor and mobile air defense, and Hyderabad finances heavy prototypes and short-run export systems. These are competing Indian lineages, not one surviving Soviet equipment tree. The Northern Indian War makes chemical warfare a demonstrated battlefield threat and turns sealing, filtered overpressure, cooling, detection, and decontamination into permanent regional design requirements.

Italy's uninterrupted tank lineage passes from the wartime P40 and victory-generation P43 Leone through the Carro 57 Ariete, stabilized Carro 68 Leone II, and composite-armored Carro 80 Ariete II. Italy adopts shared Latin 105 mm and 120 mm ammunition while retaining national vehicle, gun, engine, and fire-control programs.

Its mechanized and support systems connect infantry lift, guided antitank fire, mobile cannon air defense, 155 mm divisional artillery, and wheeled cavalry. The VCC-72 Dardo and SIDAM-76 share the Latin 25×137 mm cartridge; Palmaria and Centauro provide heavy indirect and mobile direct fire.

The Italian infantry-weapons sequence passes from a national 7.35 mm semiautomatic rifle to the common Latin 7 mm and 5.6 mm systems. The Beretta AR-82 adds the Guida Comune 79 mounting interface in the early 1980s.

Occitania's armored sequence begins with the 1946–1947 Toulouse Evacuation and Ateliers Mécaniques d’Occitanie. The Char 53 Toulouse and Char Léger 55 define its oscillating-turret period; the Char 65 Aquitaine and Char 80 Aigle return to conventional turrets and the shared 105 mm and 120 mm Latin gun generations.

Naval warfare

Germany builds the world's leading submarine system: quiet conventional boats, nuclear attack submarines, and submerged vertical-launch missile boats for conventional strikes on ports, airfields, rail, fuel, radar, and fleets. Japan and America possess the largest carrier forces.

Britain is the premier Commonwealth surface and anti-submarine navy. Italy and Spain operate peer Latin fleets. Portugal maintains an Atlantic–Indian Ocean escort navy. Occitania supplies technical experiments. Argentina fields a complete regional navy rather than a token force.

Associated-state military building is equally important to the political order. The Philippine armed forces show how older Japanese equipment can become a sovereign national institution. The Independence Fleet turns eight transferred hulls into training, patrol, relief, and naval-command capacity, while the 1st Philippine Armored Brigade uses Chi-Ha, Ha-Go, and Ho-To vehicles as a mobile reserve rather than pretending they equal modern great-power armor.

Air and orbital warfare

Germany keeps distinct interceptor, air-superiority, strike, close-support, and maritime families. Japan combines naval aviation, data links, compact aircraft, and long-range anti-ship weapons. Britain preserves TSR-2, Harrier, and the Canadian Arrow because it cannot rely on American supply.

American aerospace doctrine retains three services: Army Air Corps for battlefield aviation, Naval Air Corps for carrier and maritime aviation, and the independent United States Aerospace Force for strategic interception, bombing, reconnaissance, missiles, spaceplanes, and orbital warfare. The Maracaibo Shock drives a move from atmospheric speed toward Zenith inspection and conventional orbital strike.

Germany begins advanced trials of the one-seat Rj 3 Falke in 1985. The Raumjäger program records the Wespe–Habicht–Falke sequence and the permanent-orbit system that supports it. Japan's 1986–1988 Type 48 Raiden program distributes unmanned interceptors, laser relays, passive tracking satellites, a crewed command facility, and utility and salvage tugs.
