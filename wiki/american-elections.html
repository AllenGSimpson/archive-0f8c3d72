American Presidential Elections, 1968–1996

Eight elections transform National Renewal from insurgent third party into governing majority, return power peacefully to a reconstituted opposition, and culminate in the first contingent election of the five-party republic.[e]

1968: the House election

Rockwell wins roughly forty percent of the popular vote and the largest electoral plurality, but no candidate reaches an Electoral College majority. Democrats and Republicans form the Establishment Compact; the House chooses Republican Nelson Rockefeller and the Senate chooses the Republican vice president.[a]

Rockefeller enters office legally but without a popular mandate. National Renewal turns defeat into the Stolen Mandate myth, while congressional deadlock and foreign encroachment define the Reprieve Years.[a]

1972: fusion and containment

Democrats and Republicans formally fuse.[a] The working ticket pairs Rockefeller with Henry M. Jackson, a hawkish labor Democrat, and defeats Rockwell approximately 51–49.[e] National Renewal nevertheless wins the House and approaches Senate parity.[a]

The outcome ends the old three-party order. The Free Association Amendment becomes the key bargain of the term, constitutionally blocking federal anti-discrimination law.[a]

1976: Buchanan's narrow victory

Rockwell is assassinated during the first major speech of his campaign tour in 1975. Buchanan stops retaliatory mobilization, gives the memorial address that defines lawful party discipline, and is ratified as leader.[a]

Buchanan–Phillips narrowly wins on 2 November 1976; Buchanan turns thirty-eight that day and becomes the youngest elected president. National Renewal takes a narrow House majority while Democratic-Republicans retain the Senate.[a] Exact totals remain open.[e]

1980: high tide

Growth, Orbital Port Columbia, continuous lunar occupation, computing, and low-cost hemispheric gains produce Buchanan's landslide.[e] He defeats Ronald Reagan.[a] The working result is approximately 59–41 with more than 450 electoral votes. National Renewal's working congressional totals are roughly 255–270 House seats and 56–58 senators.[e]

Reagan's defeat does not end his career. It turns him into the best-known advocate of constitutional free enterprise and prepares the opposition's ideological recovery.[a]

1984: peaceful transfer

Howard Phillips, endorsed by Buchanan, defeats old-guard and technocratic challengers and selects Louisiana governor Gabriel Landry.[a] Reagan plausibly selects Jack Kemp[e] and campaigns against political credit, party cultural power, and the leaked “Narrative Shaping” memorandum.[a]

Reagan wins the presidency.[a] With Kemp as the provisional running mate, the working result is roughly 52.5–53.5 percent and 320–340 electoral votes. National Renewal retains a narrow House majority and Democratic-Republicans take the Senate.[e] Buchanan's full cooperation establishes that the republic can remove the governing party without violence.[a]

1988: the boom election

Reagan campaigns on restored confidence, commercial opening, the Halifax Understanding, and the real prosperity of the World Market Boom. National Renewal nominates aerospace administrator Nathaniel Reed Bell with Christian-social agrarian Miriam Kincaid.[a]

Reagan wins decisively;[a] the working popular-vote result is roughly 58–41.[e] The result confirms Democratic-Republican control during prosperity but leaves Renewal's differentiated state organizations intact under the Omaha Rules.[a]

1992: last direct two-party election

The Open Door Depression vindicates Thomas Harwood's Productive Commonwealth warnings about foreign control. The Renewal convention nominates Harwood with Bell, while Democratic-Republicans select Andrew Prescott Hale and Caleb Harlan. Gabriel Landry recognizes the nomination but refuses to endorse Harwood for president.[a]

In the general election, Harwood defeats Hale and Renewal takes congressional supermajorities.[a] The working result is approximately 51.0–48.5 and 286–252 electoral votes, with roughly 300–305 House seats and 68–69 senators for National Renewal.[e] Harwood's later attempt to impose compulsory industrial organization triggers the 1993 schism, creating Constitutional Renewal, American Producers, and National Vanguard beside Harwood Renewal and the surviving Democratic-Republicans.[a]

1996: the first mature contingent election

The five-party system produces a fractured presidential field. Andrew Hale stands for the Democratic-Republicans and reaches the constitutional top three;[a] the other nominees and finalists remain open.[e]

District and proportional elector allocation prevents any majority. After several House ballots, state delegations select Andrew Hale as the bounded stability president for 1997–2001.[a] The contingent election makes House selection an expected constitutional second round and begins the mature mandate culture.[e]
