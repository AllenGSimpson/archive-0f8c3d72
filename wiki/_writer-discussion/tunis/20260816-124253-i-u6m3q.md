The earlier city record named several communities and institutions but did not explain how household status moved among them. I have added the pre-divergence Tunis migration and labor system from Julia Clancy-Smith, Mary Lewis, and the recent study of diasporic labor politics in Tunis. The inherited city joined poor Sicilian, Italian, and Maltese migrants to Tunisian workers, merchants, religious communities, municipalities, consulates, and the unequal French protectorate. Italian nationality could provide civil and legal protection without making every Italian prosperous or politically loyal.

The pre-divergence claims are marked `[p]`. The later paragraphs follow established Italian sovereignty, retained Tunisian and French offices, mixed communities, and multilingual administration. They do not fix the open municipal law, Beylical court, postwar nationality code, neighborhood plan, bank roster, population, or 1985 leadership. The exact jurisdiction of Muslim, Jewish, Christian, consular, Tunisian, French, and Italian institutions therefore remains open.

The addition treats nationality as a legal and administrative category rather than a complete social class. Municipal and police archives can count addresses, permits, associations, strikes, and taxable businesses while making informal credit, domestic labor, women's work, and cross-community kinship harder to see. The postwar Italian state inherits this selective archive. Italian community institutions can support imperial preference without proving that poor Italian workers, Tunisian households, or other urban publics consented to the new sovereign order.
