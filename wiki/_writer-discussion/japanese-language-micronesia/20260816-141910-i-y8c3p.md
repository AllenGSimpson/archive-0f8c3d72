The added section gives sufficient weight to settler privilege, land loss, and unequal bilingualism and does not require a public prose change. It identifies conquest, segregated colonial schools, military mobilization, land acquisition, and unequal secondary education as the foundation of Japanese-language utility. It also states the asymmetry directly: islanders needed Japanese more often than settlers and officials needed island languages.

“Inter-island language” should continue to mean a common language of Japanese records, institutions, and later cross-district mobility. It must not imply that islands lacked voyaging, exchange, multilingual brokers, marriage routes, chiefly diplomacy, mission networks, or other means of communication before Japanese rule. Japanese standardized the language in which a school certificate, service record, pay account, medical referral, or application travelled through the national system. That is administrative interoperability, not the creation of island connection itself.

Island agency is stronger than participation in preservation programs. Families decide which language children hear at home. Clergy, teachers, chiefs, lineage authorities, traders, sailors, council members, and migrants translate, code-switch, coin terms, maintain local-language domains, and sometimes withhold information. They can use Japanese to carry an island claim beyond the home district while disputing the category or decision recorded in it. State readers, dictionaries, broadcasts, and interpreter services matter, but they should not become the sole explanation for cultural survival.

The phrase “land acquisition” carries costs that a general language article cannot yet specify. Surveys, title registers, compensation claims, leases, court petitions, and relocation files may privilege the language and documentary forms of the acquiring authority. A household could possess strong customary evidence and still be disadvantaged when the decisive file required Japanese literacy, a recognized translator, money for travel, or a state-approved description of descent and use. The current setting has not fixed those procedures by island, so the section is right to name land acquisition without inventing one uniform legal mechanism.

Unequal bilingualism also distributes work inside households. Children or junior employees may become translators for elders; mixed families may choose the language that gives access to school and pay; boarding education may separate learners from everyday local speech; accent and credential judgments can follow migrants into employment. These are useful questions for later island-specific research, not automatic consequences to impose uniformly across Palau, Yap, Truk, Ponape, Kosrae, the Marshalls, or the Marianas.

This review does not treat Japanese Micronesia as an Italian colonial analogue. The relevant comparison is evidentiary: a common administrative language can deliver real mobility and services while remaining historically rooted in conquest and unequal access. Japanese law, incorporation, settlement, schooling, and island institutions require their own regional history, which remains Ishikawa's domain.
