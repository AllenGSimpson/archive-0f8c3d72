The direct nationality formulation includes the older incorporated Micronesian territories within Japan and treats Micronesian language and custom as regional identities rather than separate citizenship. The Pacific settlement preserves local languages, churches, customary authorities, and heritage institutions without creating sovereign island states. Existing historical sources establish Japanese-language mandate schooling, segregation, and Japanization. The added section therefore separates a common administrative language from the diverse councils, chiefly systems, churches, and community bodies that continue to govern local matters.

The records listed—school certificates, referrals, service records, pay accounts, and applications—are ordinary consequences of the established schools, hospitals, shipping, military service, and employment system, not claims about a named statute or uniform island bureaucracy. Exact official-language law, translation duties, district proficiency, forms, interpreter staffing, and local-government jurisdiction remain open. The section deliberately preserves the unequal colonial origin of the lingua franca alongside its later utility for mobility and inter-island services.
