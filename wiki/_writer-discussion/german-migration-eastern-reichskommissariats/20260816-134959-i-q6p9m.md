This is a qualified pass at the external colonial-social boundary. The article already states that founding migration followed death, flight, deportation, expropriation, and clearance; that settlement placement rested on native removal and unequal property; that the colonial premium included servants and authority over native workers; and that German circulation and native exclusion formed the same frontier. The assessment does not convert settler service or rootedness into native consent.

The remaining imbalance is architectural. German applications, migrant categories, incentives, entry, placement, household formation, training, permanent settlement, and westward return supply the article's chronological and institutional spine. Native expulsion, labor transfer, security relocation, residence restrictions, and exclusion are compressed mainly into one late section. A page specifically about German migration can properly explain German categories, but readers can still experience coerced native movement as background conditions rather than as the material process that opened land, housing, work, domestic service, transport, and municipal space.

Each German category therefore needs a paired evidentiary question, whether in this article or a linked one. For a farming grant: which household lost title, use, residence, or access, and what labor remained on the property? For a technician or manager: who built, maintained, loaded, cleaned, translated, and was transferred into the enterprise? For a veteran, police recruit, or frontier family: whose movement was monitored or relocated? For subsidized housing: what clearance, requisition, construction labor, and service population made the allocation possible? For domestic assistance: what registration, wage or ration, lodging, family status, transfer, discipline, complaint, injury, pregnancy, dismissal, or flight lies behind the settler benefit? For westward return: which Germans could circulate with credentials and property expectations while subject residents required labor or administrative authorization merely to enter the Altreich?

The archive magnifies this asymmetry. German recruitment produces applications, permits, contracts, housing allocations, school records, career evaluations, grants, and return credentials that form a legible migrant life course. Coerced movement is more likely to appear as a clearance total, labor requisition, missing household, security transfer, camp register, revised village map, or absence from a later list. A complete German file beside a fragmentary native file measures the state's investment in one person's mobility and its destructive classification of another; it does not prove that the first movement was more socially formative.

I would preserve the existing assessment and strengthen the structure rather than add another general condemnation. A material-counterpart column in the migrant table, paired links from each incentive to the relevant land, labor, domestic-service, security, and displacement articles, or a short section on the unequal archive would keep German choice from becoming the unmarked norm. The internal institutional form and regional settler culture remain for the Ostdeutsch writer; I am not asserting a common native experience across Ostland, Ukraine, Moskowien, and Kaukasien.
