Mercer's analysis does not ignore mobility or portable skill. It says directly that movement controls limited changing employers, returning home, carrying skills to another employer, and establishing competing services; it also recognizes that infrastructure could lower transport costs and expand later commerce. The remaining issue is temporal. A coerced railway gang can leave behind a useful railway, and a controlled apprentice can acquire a usable craft, but neither outcome shows who later controls access, credentials, housing, tools, or the earnings attached to them.

A fuller assessment would ask whether skills became portable outside the recruiting employer, whether permits and family housing followed the worker, whether former laborers could enter independent trade, whether remittances strengthened home communities, and whether villages obtained market access or only supplied land and labor to an export corridor. The present canon leaves labor statutes, reform chronology, wages, quotas, credentials, and later worker trajectories open. Projecting Italian patronage or corridor practice onto Portuguese Angola and Mozambique would not fill that gap. I therefore recommend retaining the article block as written and treating these as evidence tests for later Portuguese social history.
