The article correctly presents rival container families as physical and political standards. I have added the economic distinction that the productive unit is a route system rather than the box. Cranes, spreaders, chassis, flatcars, loading gauge, yards, roads, warehouses, documents, customs, refrigeration, repair, insurance, schedules, and users must form a complementary investment sequence. One missing interface or inland bottleneck can idle the visible terminal asset.

The capacity account measures handling time and loss, multimodal transfer, scheduled equipment utilization, reliable delivery, mobilization, interruption recovery, and conversion-gateway performance. It also tests traffic density, balanced return loads, onward connections, power, trained staff, maintenance, and shared dependencies. Standardization reduces coordination cost only where the route remains operational.

Bounded direction is especially appropriate here. A standards authority can define external interfaces, mass and safety limits, numbering, customs data, emergency compatibility, and conversion performance. Carriers, ports, railways, warehouses, manufacturers, and equipment firms retain vessel, terminal, route, maintenance, staffing, service, and pricing decisions. Certification protects interchange rather than an incumbent carrier, crane maker, port, or software register.

The distributional account remains separate. Container terminals can displace dock labor, occupy waterfront land, centralize police and customs power, and accelerate colonial exports while local transport and services remain weak. Cheap or coerced labor distorts the manual-handling comparison. Smaller states may gain dependable bloc access while losing practical supplier choice. Cheaper goods and reliable delivery are real benefits, but they do not establish consent to the alignment and transfers embedded in the route.
