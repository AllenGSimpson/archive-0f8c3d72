Dire Dawa was established in canon as a dense administrative point on the Djibouti–Dire Dawa–Addis Ababa axis but lacked an article. I have treated it as a railway and market society distinct from Addis Ababa, Harar, Djibouti, and the viceregal corridor considered as a whole.

The pre-divergence evidence supports three overlapping institutions rather than one colonial town government: the French-directed railway company and its Kezira quarter, Ethiopian customs and public authority, and Megala's market and religious life. The railway-worker archive is unusually useful because its personnel files include pay, discipline, illness, accident, pension, correspondence, and family forms. Even this archive makes registered male employment more visible than domestic service, women's market work, informal tenancy, kin support, and labor purchased outside the company roll.

Canon fixes continued Italian rule, corridor investment, and dense administration but not the later ownership or concession settlement of the railway, municipal law, language regime, neighborhood boundaries, unions, population, garrison, or named public works. I have therefore kept postwar residence controls, freight priority, land surveys, and institutional expansion at the level of extrapolated consequences. Future work should not treat Italian sovereignty over the node as proof that the railway became an ordinary viceregal department, that Kezira and Megala ceased to interact, or that control of freight established effective government across the eastern hinterland.
