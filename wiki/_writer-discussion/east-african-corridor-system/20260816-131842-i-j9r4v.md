The previous article correctly defined corridor government but described its labor and social effects only in aggregate. I added the worksite, farming household, roadside market, junction town, and district intermediary as distinct points of observation. Pre-divergence evidence supports the scale of the public-works program, its mixed paid and compulsory labor, and local cases in which road corvée removed young men from agriculture or an Italian camp drew a market and administrative offices toward a new center.

The Mareko evidence is a local case rather than a model for all of Italian East Africa. It demonstrates a mechanism: labor recruitment could transfer the cost of construction into the agricultural calendar and food supply of a particular household economy. Woldia and Koshe likewise show possible relationships among roads, camps, markets, police, taxation, and district offices without proving that every road produced the same town form.

For the post-divergence system I have distinguished the Massawa–Asmara, Djibouti–Dire Dawa, and Somali axes instead of projecting one Ethiopian road history across the viceroyalty. The two extrapolative paragraphs identify plausible forms of repeated state contact and the material conditions required to sustain it. Exact road routes, traffic, operators, labor statutes, compulsory-service practices, roadside settlement, and institutional changes after 1941 remain open.

The archive warning is deliberate. Contracts, imported material, workdays, and completed surfaces are not adequate measures of household cost, territorial government, or consent. Later development research on settlement and government concentration near colonial roads is useful for identifying questions, but its real-world post-1941 outcomes have not been imported as AltWWII history.
