This new article treats defeat without occupation as a problem of lawful command rather than military dissolution. Its evidence comes from the Chiefs of Staff assessment and constitutional transfer in 1947, the Release and Resettlement Scheme, the retained force mix, Commonwealth Council limits, the Indian Operations Commission and 1952 White Paper, Kuwait, Second Torch, No Third Torch, the Runway Election, and the Falklands Commission. The article distinguishes the Crown's institutional place from personal royal command, professional advice from a military veto, operational delegation from ministerial responsibility, and assigned Commonwealth command from authority over another realm's forces. Exact service law, commissions and oaths, defence-ministry organization, appointments, establishments, mobilization law, and the complete command machinery remain open. Operational judgments about planning, equipment, tactics, and commander performance are deliberately left to Langley; he should test whether the constitutional distinctions preserve rather than distort professional command.
