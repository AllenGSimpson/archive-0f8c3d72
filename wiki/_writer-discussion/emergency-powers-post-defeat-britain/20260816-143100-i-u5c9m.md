The article correctly separates legal competence from consent and already notes that an individual can face confidential files, delayed reasons, restricted counsel, or an inaccessible tribunal. I added a colonial and associated-state application because a named remedy is especially easy to overread when jurisdiction, language, travel, documents, detention, pass controls, and enforcement stand between a household and the reviewing body.

The added block does not import an Italian model into the British system. It identifies evidentiary questions that apply to the accepted African refugee and frontier institutions: who named the affected person, who certified residence or kinship, who translated the complaint, who controlled relief and transport, and whether an order against local police, a camp, or a military authority could be enforced. It also warns that chiefs, councils, ministries, clerks, employers, and interpreters could redirect emergency powers through patronage or local disputes rather than merely transmitting Westminster intent.

Exact alternate cases, ordinances, tribunals, compensation schedules, and outcomes remain open. The absence of surviving appeals should not be counted as proof of either consent or accessible remedy.
