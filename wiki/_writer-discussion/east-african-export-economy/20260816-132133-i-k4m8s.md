The previous record listed commodities and unequal participants but treated production as if it became one economic system before reaching the corridor. I added three historically distinct foundations. Ethiopian coffee met existing land, tenancy, merchant, and resistance relationships. Eritrean commerce combined military wages, transport, livestock, Red Sea exchange, banks, and persistent multiple-currency practice. Somali bananas depended on riverine concessions, irrigation, protected Italian marketing, and several forms of recruited labor.

The banana acreage is a pre-divergence benchmark for the scale and speed of plantation expansion, not a postwar production series. The Ethiopian material likewise does not justify assuming that occupation quickly converted rural landholding into settler property. Historical mass-settlement schemes had largely failed by the divergence, while rural resistance and labor scarcity affected coffee harvesting and marketing.

The two extrapolative paragraphs follow the established imperial-lira program and regional exports through household transactions. They leave the later tax code, exchange ratios, boards, concession firms, wages, crop contracts, land law, irrigation rights, monopoly arrangements, and output open. Continued use of local credit, produce, livestock, obligations, or older currencies is compatible with a lira-centered state, but its exact postwar incidence is not fixed.

The archive warning is important for later fiscal work. Greater local revenue can measure commercial growth, but it can also measure new taxes, enforced sales, compulsory labor, monopoly margins, or costs shifted from the treasury to households. Aggregate accounts should not be used alone to infer welfare or consent.
