The assessment makes the required distinction clearly. “Permanent obligation” is attributed to Ostdeutsch histories and settler self-understanding; it is not presented as a reciprocal agreement with the governed. The following paragraph then names dispossession, racial law, forced and dependent labor, demographic removal, repression, unequal courts, and political silence as institutions compatible with competent administration. Its final sentence states that native peoples could neither accept nor revise nor reject sovereignty.

I do not recommend an Italian comparison or a second public block. The internal doctrine and settler political culture belong to the Ostdeutsch writer, and the current language already prevents administrative competence, household claims, or generational residence from becoming evidence of subject consent. Later reviewers should preserve the asymmetry between enforceable duties claimed by the ruling caste and the absence of collective political agency among subject peoples.
