The Kismayo article previously began with its generic role as a southern port and then moved directly to the Mogadishu corridor and San Marco. I added the local and institutional history that the aerospace city inherited: the Oltregiuba transfer, Kismayo's brief High Commission, colonial jurisdiction, Bajuni coastal society, lower-Juba agriculture, pastoral movement, and coerced plantation labor.

The 1925 judicial decree provides unusually specific evidence about administrative reach. It extended Italian Somalia's judicial order to Oltregiuba, seated a regional tribunal at Chisimaio, recognized councils of indigenous elders, preserved a differentiated competence for qadis, and placed appeals through the resident and High Commissioner. The decree did not merely leave custom untouched. The High Commissioner recognized the councils and defined their jurisdiction; the regional court could include a local notable only with a consultative vote. The new prose treats this as the colonial conversion of local authority into a subordinate legal procedure.

The social table separates four connected settings rather than treating Jubaland as one Somali population: the Kismayo–Bajuni coast, lower-Juba river settlements, pastoral hinterland, and port town. The categories identify livelihoods, movement, mediators, and the office or employer through which colonial power appeared. They are analytical settings, not fixed ethnic territories or a postwar census.

The Bajuni material establishes a coastal community whose history, Kibajuni language, fishing, cultivation, trade, and seamanship predated San Marco. Somali and Swahili circulation is included without imposing a later language hierarchy. Arabic religious and commercial practice is left implicit because the present sources do not establish the language balance of individual institutions in the postwar city.

The labor paragraph distinguishes a contract or wage entry from free labor. Historical research on Italian Somalia shows that development planning institutionalized forced labor, disrupted food production, and targeted riverine communities descended from enslaved and servile populations. Guards, flight, coercive recruitment, and the scheme remembered as <i>colonya</i> must be considered alongside wages and rations.

Because Italian East Africa survives, the historical British occupation and subsequent Somali independence do not supply a later land or labor settlement. No claim is made that every prewar concession or coercive practice persisted unchanged. Postwar labor law, concessions, land tenure, irrigation administration, union rights, freedom of departure, and municipal government remain open.

The final paragraph places San Marco over this older landscape. It identifies land, water, electricity, fishing access, instruction, apprenticeships, screening, and compensation as the evidence needed to determine which households benefited. Technical employment and infrastructure are not treated as proof that port, coastal, riverine, or pastoral communities consented to Italian sovereignty.
