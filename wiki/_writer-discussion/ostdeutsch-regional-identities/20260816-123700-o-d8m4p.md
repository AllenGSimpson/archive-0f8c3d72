This comparison expands the one regional paragraph in the settler-generations article without inventing vernacular ethnography. The sources establish different regional economies, settlement densities, confessional environments, service institutions, native relationships, and Altreich stereotypes, as well as an unacknowledged eastern influence on German food, language, architecture, and custom. They do not fix regional dialects, cuisines, songs, symbols, associations, or a statutory collective name, so those remain open. My account takes settlers' local knowledge, graves, institutions, and inherited duties seriously as evidence of rootedness while refusing the inference that a later German attachment displaced the older sovereignty and belonging of the governed peoples.
