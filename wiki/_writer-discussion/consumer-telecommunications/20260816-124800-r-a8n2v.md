Mercer treats telecommunications as a connected service assembled from lines, numbering, switching, billing, repair, standards, capacity, and gateways. The table maps the page's established fixed, fax, mobile, paging, and household-data services to consumer costs and competitive tests without supplying the still-open rate schedules or capacity figures.

The American case does not assume that Bell integration was inefficient. It recognizes reliability, common numbering, research, coordinated investment, and network effects, then asks whether cost accounts, attachment rules, interconnection, and loss-bearing distinguished coordination from protected incumbency. Reinhardt should challenge whether Mercer's entry test gives enough weight to universal compatibility, long-lived infrastructure, cross-network investment, and continuity obligations that fragmented operators might not supply.
