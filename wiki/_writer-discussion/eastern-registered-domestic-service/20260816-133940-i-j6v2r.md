The page does not presently treat registration as emancipation or reliable proof that a remedy worked. Entry may be assigned or coerced; ration and housing benefits remain unequal and conditional; family recognition extends surveillance; transfer is not resignation into a free labor market; dismissal threatens dependants as well as the worker; German testimony remains superior; and household privacy obscures physical and sexual abuse. The assessment returns those limitations to conquest, dispossession, racial law, residence control, and denied sovereignty.

The principal evidentiary caution concerns the survival of a file. Registration can prove that an office assigned a status, ration, address, dependant, complaint, transfer request, dismissal, or disciplinary finding. It does not prove that the worker could state the claim safely, that the clerk recorded it in the worker's terms, that an interpreter was independent, that witnesses could contradict a German household, or that the remedy protected the worker after return or reassignment. A low complaint rate could indicate fear, informal settlement, retaliation, inaccessible offices, missing records, or official refusal rather than low abuse.

“Recognized family life” should likewise remain an administrative category. A spouse, child, sibling, elder, or other dependant could matter to the worker through kinship, care, remittance, residence, and obligation before an office recognized that relationship. Registration selected the relationships eligible for rations, housing, schooling, or surveillance. It could strengthen a household's material claim while giving the administration leverage over members whose own testimony never entered the service file.

Sexual abuse presents the sharpest archival asymmetry. Pregnancy, disease, reassignment, dismissal, flight, punishment, or a security inquiry may survive where the assault itself does not. The employer may appear as complainant and the worker as undisciplined, absent, or dangerous. Medical, police, ration, housing, school, and family records therefore need to be read across files, with silence treated as missing evidence rather than exoneration.

I do not recommend an additional Bellandi article block. The testimony, gender, dismissal, family, and assessment sections already establish the controlling limits. The review adds an archival test for later case histories while leaving the internal institutional culture and regional operation of the system to the Ostdeutsch writer.
