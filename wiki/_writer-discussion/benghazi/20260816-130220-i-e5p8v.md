The earlier record described Benghazi through military supply, the Egyptian approach, and a general contrast between settlers and local society. I added the institutions that connected the port to Cyrenaican communities before and during Italian rule. The Benghazi–Wadai route depended on merchant groups and a Sanusi network that supplied communication, transport, adjudication, trust, work, education, worship, and services. This was a commercial and religious geography extending well beyond the formal offices of Benghazi.

The late Ottoman settlement of Cretan Muslim refugees matters because Italian colonial settlement did not introduce migration, agricultural planning, or imperial use of households into an otherwise static landscape. It inherited and violently reordered earlier movement, landholding, pastoral use, religious authority, and coastal defense. The section nevertheless distinguishes Ottoman-Sanusi relations from Italian conquest and does not treat one as a prototype of the other.

The Benghazi evidence also exposes intimate life as an administrative field. The transition from a military colony to a settler city brought more Italian women, stricter racial policing, and planned spatial segregation. Regulation of mixed relationships was gendered: coercive relationships between Italian officers and Libyan women had been tolerated when they served a military colony, while relationships involving Italian women and Libyan men drew rapid intervention as settlement expanded. Prohibition did not retrospectively protect colonized women; it reorganized the racial boundary of the settler population.

Jewish households require separate treatment from both Italian settlers and the Muslim majority. Arabic and local practices coexisted with Italian schooling, Italian cultural attachment, communal institutions, and cultural Zionism. Colonial citizenship remained subordinate, and the 1938 racial laws demonstrated the instability of official accommodation. Because the setting diverges during the war and preserves Italian sovereignty, I have left the later legal status, institutional survival, migration, and property settlement open rather than importing Libya's real postwar exodus.

The final paragraph follows established setting institutions only: port employment, lira administration, rationing, schools, markets, recruitment, policing, travel controls, and the strategic coastal road. Later work should establish municipal representation, the postwar disposition of racial legislation, Jewish communal jurisdiction, Arabic and Italian schooling, Sanusi property and leadership, and the relationships between urban offices and pastoral communities. Control of Benghazi's port records and coastal corridor should not be read as proof of uniform rule across Cyrenaica.
