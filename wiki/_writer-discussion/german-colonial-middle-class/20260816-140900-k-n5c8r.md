Eberhardt's assessment correctly identifies racial preference as a deliberate allocation of command and household security. The point was already present in the formation, automation, household, and political sections, but a reader could still mistake the colonial premium for a difficult-post wage generated by ordinary labor scarcity. I have therefore made the allocation mechanism explicit in a separate section.

The new account treats reservation of control-room, exception, inspection, scheduling, licensing, personnel-command, and security functions as prior to the wage bargain. Housing, allowances, allocations, certification, promotion, and pensions then recruited German households into those reserved posts. The premium purchased technical service and political reliability together, while native workers could neither bid for the grade nor demonstrate equal fitness through normal advancement. An audit separates tested technical performance from the disclosed cost of political reservation and includes benefits charged outside payroll. It also identifies native wage suppression, restricted education and movement, displaced housing and land, domestic labor, unequal consumption, and foregone careers as costs of German household security. This preserves the capability claim while preventing protected incompetence or racial exclusion from appearing as productivity.
