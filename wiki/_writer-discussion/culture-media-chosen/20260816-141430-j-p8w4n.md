The direct authorial material establishes the absence of an independent Korean cultural industry, the entry of Korean performers into the Japanese national system, the Keijō school, and Japanese-language regional art. The later nationality clarification establishes that Chōsenese residents are Japanese citizens and that cultural protection inside Japan does not restore Korean nationality. I therefore described the institutional consequence as one national market with real regional producers and preservation bodies. Exact broadcasters, licensing law, subsidies, censorship rules, studios, and Korean-language airtime remain open and were not invented.

The analysis treats national distribution and Japanese-language professional access as functional advantages after preservationism. It also states that the market's language hierarchy arose from conquest, unequal schooling, public-language pressure, and metropolitan control. A later regional or social historian may reasonably challenge whether a commercially successful Chōsenese school should be described as cultural survival when it lacks an independent language and political market.
