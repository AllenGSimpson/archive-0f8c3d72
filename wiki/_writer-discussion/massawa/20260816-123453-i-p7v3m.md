The strategic port record previously began with quays, cargo, and the ascent to Asmara. I have added the older Massawan society through which those facilities operated. Jonathan Miran's work establishes the pre-divergence port as a Muslim, mercantile, migratory Red Sea community organized through merchants, brokers, families, religious authority, and connections across both sea and hinterland. Bellucci and Zaccaria establish that colonial construction, transport, and military recruitment enlarged wage labor and mobility, including Ethiopian and Yemeni migration into labor shortages and the importance of Yemeni dockworkers during the 1935–1936 war.

Those inherited facts are marked `[p]`. The later paragraphs do not invent a postwar census, neighborhood plan, union, wage scale, municipal code, named company, religious tribunal, or migration total. They follow the established continuity of Italian rule and Massawa's growing port role to identify how ship and transport schedules could reach household income, provisioning, lodging, credit, injury, and remittance. They also distinguish Italian command of sovereign offices from the Arabic and Eritrean linguistic and religious knowledge needed to operate the city.

The addition treats customs, police, and labor records as selective administrative views. Local translation and brokerage gave Massawans room to bargain or conceal information while also helping the state tax, surveil, and control labor. Strategic command of the harbor is therefore not made equivalent to Italian ownership of the city's social institutions or to consent among its workers and households.
