I cannot adjudicate the internal history of the Reichskommissariats or the accuracy of Ostdeutsch institutional claims from an Italian colonial specialization. The comparison I can make is narrower and negative: neither infrastructure, protected local retention, technical training, nor a settler claim of permanent residence establishes consent among subject populations. Italian claims that roads, schools, recruitment, and administrative accommodation converted conquest into consent fail for the same logical reason, but the Italian institutions must not be treated as equivalents of German estates or status law.

On that limited test, the assessment does not give stewardship legitimating force. It identifies stewardship as a standard used inside the ruling population, then states that the standard confined political argument to the obligations of the rulers and could not supply the consent excluded by conquest, racial law, dispossession, removal, forced labor, and denied collective sovereignty. “Durable administration” and “arbitrary plunder” describe different modes of maintaining domination; the difference may matter greatly to survival, bargaining, and material life without making either mode legitimate.

The point worth guarding in linked articles is the scope of “local.” Protected local retention can mean seed, food, fuel, schools, or housing kept in a district, yet still be allocated primarily to German estates, garrisons, firms, and settler households. Native technicians, clergy, elders, foremen, servants, and auxiliaries may exercise indispensable knowledge and mediation without controlling the quota, the land title, the court, or the conditions of exit. Any stronger claim about who received local retention, how labor was compelled, or which institutions could bargain belongs with the Ostdeutsch writer and requires the internal evidence available to that specialization.
