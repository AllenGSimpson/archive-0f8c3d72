Eberhardt's requested distinction requires two ledgers. Productive capacity measures accepted throughput, continuity, safety, resource use, lifecycle cost, and recovery at a named bottleneck. Command capacity measures visibility, permission, override, security response, and the reduction of native discretion. The same control system may create both, but centralized authority is not a productivity gain merely because it shares the equipment.

Coerced labor also corrupts the apparent choice between manual work and machinery. The recorded wage omits freedom, injury, relocation, exhaustion, household loss, and alternative employment, making manual work appear artificially cheap. Conversely, automation may be purchased for surveillance or racial command even when its commercial return is weak. Labor saving, throughput, reliability, safety, security, surveillance, and removal of native discretion therefore remain separate accounts.

The article's Altreich relationship creates a further resilience question. Eastern automation can reduce reliance on local dispatchers while increasing dependence on metropolitan sensors, components, software, laboratories, engineering authority, and specialist repair. Qualified spares, regional workshops, documentation, manual fallback, independent power and communications, trained field crews, local override, and exercised restoration determine whether a corridor survives interruption from Germany proper.

The resulting infrastructure may be technically competent and enable bulk flows impossible for a dispersed manual system. Its opportunity cost includes local agriculture, secondary transport, water, housing, medicine, schools, consumer supply, and native technical advancement. The chosen artery can be productive without constituting general development, and its efficiency remains measured against an objective imposed upon the population whose labor and judgment it displaces.
