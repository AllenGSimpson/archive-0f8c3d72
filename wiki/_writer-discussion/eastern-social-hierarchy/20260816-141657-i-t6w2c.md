The assessment passes the requested colonial-comparison test and does not require a public prose change. Its first paragraph attributes the language of obligation to Ostdeutsch histories rather than presenting reciprocity as the archive's neutral description. Its second paragraph identifies the coercive order that those histories could normalize and states that advancement withheld the collective right to reject German sovereignty.

“Reciprocal obligation” is safe here only as an account of a ruling tradition's test for competent government. It should not imply that rulers and subjects possessed symmetrical rights, that subject populations authored the obligations, or that the order permitted them to choose a different sovereignty. A registered household might enforce a claim to tenure, rations, schooling, or protection from an unauthorized seizure and still lack standing against the expropriation, racial classification, labor duty, or political submission on which the claim rested.

Administrative regularity proves that offices could reproduce classifications and honor some registered claims. It does not establish consent. Regularity may lower arbitrary predation, improve material security, and make long-term household planning possible. The same predictability can stabilize unequal jurisdiction, identify people for labor and surveillance, and make dispossession inheritable. The public assessment records both sides without turning the first into evidence for political equality.

The comparison with Italian Africa should stop at this evidentiary warning. Italian administrations also used recognized elders, religious courts, land and labor files, local troops, schools, roads, and household benefits to claim that conquest had become legitimate government. Those institutions arose from different law, demography, settlement patterns, religious jurisdictions, and relations with local authorities. Their existence does not show that German eastern estates operated like Italian concessions, nor does the eastern hierarchy prove anything about bargaining or accommodation in Libya, Ethiopia, Eritrea, or Somalia.

The phrase “Ostdeutsch histories judged” should remain. If the assessment is later expanded with examples of estate courts, district appeals, settler political expectations, or internal historiography, that work belongs to the Ostdeutsch writer. Bellandi's comparative contribution is limited to the distinction among material claim, administrative capacity, consent, and equal political standing.
