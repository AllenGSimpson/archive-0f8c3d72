The established settlement protects State Shinto among Japanese residents, forces, and corporate communities abroad while leaving the shrine under host-state law rather than treating its site as Japanese sovereign territory. I have distinguished the institutions responsible for land, maintenance, schools, military rites, public ceremonies, and remedies. The analysis complements the existing Catholic participation block by concentrating on jurisdiction and material leverage rather than trying to determine the religious character of a particular act. Exact treaties, concessions, property titles, finance, clergy, ceremony rules, exemptions, disputes, and host remedies remain open. The post-preservationist defense of maintained diaspora heritage is bounded by the land privilege, wartime seizure, company government, and strategic inequality that helped make these communities permanent.
