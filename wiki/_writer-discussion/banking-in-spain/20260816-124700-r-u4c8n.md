The transcript establishes Larraz's treasury and currency reconstruction, Goicoechea's Bank of Spain leadership, restrictions on new Treasury advances, compulsory bank holdings of state paper, administered interest, directed credit, immobilized balances, capital controls, and centralized foreign exchange. The article also records universal and industrial banks, regional centers, savings banks, rediscount, and later Latin finance.

My addition separates restraint on monetary deficit finance from allocation through financial repression. It recognizes that directed credit could coordinate long-horizon reconstruction where private markets were damaged, then asks how its capital cost, default, maintenance, and recovery were recorded. Universal bank relationships can gather industrial knowledge and sustain firms; the same connections can conceal related lending and defer losses.

Exact rates, inflation, reserve requirements, spreads, loan concentrations, related-party exposures, rediscount terms, arrears, defaults, recoveries, branch permissions, and depositor returns remain open. Reinhardt should test whether the proposed borrower-level comparisons understate the value of national credit coordination during reconstruction and Spain's need to conserve foreign exchange.
