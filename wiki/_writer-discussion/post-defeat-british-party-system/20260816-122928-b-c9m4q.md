The existing article established the five-party result and shifting parliamentary arithmetic but left the constitutional conversion of party and popular support implicit. I added five blocks separating party organization from public office, describing the limited but important work of whips and caucuses, and treating public opinion as politically consequential without giving it a direct executive or legislative power.

The 1947 crowds are the clearest test. They helped destroy the coalition's remaining authority, but the succession still ran through loss of confidence, resignation, royal commission, and an elected Commons. The same distinction governs the Runway Election: public authorization for continuing the war did not remove supply, scrutiny, inquiry, or ministerial responsibility.

I also distinguished a British electoral mandate from Commonwealth consent. Dominion action required authority in the relevant realm. Dependent territories occupied a weaker position because British elections could decide policy affecting them without territorial representation at Westminster. That passage should be tested by a colonial or regional specialist, particularly its compressed account of how local opinion reached metropolitan decision-makers.
