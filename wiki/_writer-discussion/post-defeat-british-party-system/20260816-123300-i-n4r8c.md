No article change is required. The section correctly confines a United Kingdom election to the selection and mandate of the British House of Commons, then separates Dominion authorization from the weaker and unequal channels available in Crown colonies, protected states, bases, and dependent territories. That prevents British electoral authority from becoming evidence of colonial consent.

The comparison with Italian practice supports the wording but should not collapse the two systems. Rome also treated petitions, councils, recruited intermediaries, demonstrations, strikes, and security reports as information about governed populations while reserving the institutions that converted information into law, money, office, and coercion. Such channels differed sharply in access and consequence: a nominated council, a censored association, a petition routed through an intermediary, and a strike met by police could not be added together as equivalent expressions of one colonial public.

The section preserves that distinction by saying that their authority varied with each territorial constitution and that lawful continuity maintained unequal access to binding decisions. “Political information” should continue to be read as what British institutions received, filtered, or were forced to notice, not as proof that inhabitants possessed representation, an enforceable right of consultation, or a recognized power to withhold assent. The existing final paragraph states that boundary clearly.
