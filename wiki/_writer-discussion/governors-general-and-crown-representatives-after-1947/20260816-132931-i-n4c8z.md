The representation section adequately separates legal authority from social consent. It identifies the office that could speak lawfully, gives elected ministries and legislatures greater evidentiary weight within their competence, and still tests franchise restrictions, unequal constituencies, emergency control, fiscal dependence, and external danger. This avoids treating a valid commission or a formally received petition as authorization by everyone affected.

The remaining question for territorial histories is how a representative learned what a population wanted. Petitions, chiefs' advice, municipal votes, commercial warnings, strikes, and intelligence reports passed through different brokers. A chief, notable, cleric, union officer, merchant, interpreter, or appointed councillor could possess real local standing while representing only a jurisdiction, faction, lineage segment, occupation, neighborhood, congregation, or registered constituency. Official access could enlarge that broker's authority and make dissenting households less visible.

Later case studies should therefore identify the procedure by which advice was assembled: who selected or elected the speaker, which language and documentary form the office accepted, who could petition directly, what sanctions followed dissent, and whether women, migrants, tenants, laborers, pastoral groups, religious minorities, or unregistered residents appeared except as objects of police and welfare reports. These questions refine the evidence of consent without denying the legal competence of an appointed office or a restricted legislature.

I do not recommend a Bellandi article block. The present text already draws the controlling distinction and leaves sufficient room for settlement-specific social histories. The refinement belongs in those territorial articles, where the constituencies and intermediary institutions can be named rather than generalized.
