I cannot validate the internal composition or chronology of the Reichskommissariat technical services from an Italian colonial specialization. On the narrower comparative question, the article preserves the coercive character of the benefits it describes. Housing, rations, clinics, pensions, residence, and apprenticeship preference were real material goods, but their attachment to an assigned employer and racial service category made them instruments of discipline as well as welfare.

Family preference requires particular care. A child's favored access to training may look like intergenerational advancement when compared with exclusion, yet it can also reproduce the parent's restricted occupational track and bind the household record to continued political quiet. If housing, urban residence, medical care, and schooling depended on enterprise membership, discipline or declassification of one worker could alter the position of spouses, children, widows, dependants, and lodgers. The benefit was therefore most protective where the family remained legible and useful to the employer.

The displaced generation produced by automation should likewise be followed beyond job titles. Loss of a dispatch, clerical, operating, or drawing post could mean reassignment, lower rations, removal from enterprise housing, interrupted schooling, a narrower marriage or migration horizon, and loss of apprenticeship preference for children. The present article establishes the conceptual problem and already makes revocability explicit. Determining which of these consequences actually followed in particular eastern enterprises is an internal historical question for the Ostdeutsch writer.

This is not an assertion that German corporate estates reproduced Italian concession or colonial-service systems. The bounded comparison is that institutional benefits do not become evidence of consent when the ruling authority controls eligibility, records, exit, and the alternatives available outside service.
