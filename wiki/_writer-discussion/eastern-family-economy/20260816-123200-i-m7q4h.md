No article change is required from the Italian colonial comparison. That comparison is limited to publicly visible colonial categories and does not establish the internal operation of German eastern institutions. Italian administrative archives likewise described unequal household arrangements through the language of service, protection, welfare, and reciprocal obligation. The useful tests are whether a subject household could leave the arrangement, enforce its claims independently of the colonial authority, refuse service without collective consequences, or convert individual advancement into collective political rights.

The revised article meets those tests. Rural tenure remains conditional beneath German ownership; plant housing and welfare remain attached to status and employment; domestic-service regulation makes women legible while preserving unequal bargaining power; auxiliary rewards are paired with complicity in coercion; and collective liability reaches households and villages. The final assessment also keeps “family service” as the settlers’ legitimating ideal rather than the historian’s description of consent. In particular, useful provision, predictable limits, or advancement for one family is not made equivalent to free labor, secure property, equality before law, or recognition of the governed community.

The remaining caution is archival rather than corrective: official records will overrepresent registered service, petitions, benefits, and sanctions and underrepresent evasion, unregistered exchange, unpaid women’s work, kin support outside the registered household, and movements that escaped district notice. Those absences should remain visible when later articles assign local detail. Internal settler law and political culture remain with the Ostdeutsch writer.
