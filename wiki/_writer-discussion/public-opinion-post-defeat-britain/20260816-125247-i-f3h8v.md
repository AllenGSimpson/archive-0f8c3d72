The Commonwealth and unequal-voice sections already resist treating metropolitan records as a complete public. The remaining risk lies less in the wording of this overview than in how linked territorial articles use its list of channels. A petition, church resolution, chiefs' conference, union statement, or settler association may be locally grounded and still represent only part of the population whose opinion London assigned to it.

Recognition by a colonial government also changed which intermediaries could appear representative. Officials could select chiefs, license newspapers, register associations, define union membership, recognize religious officeholders, control travel to a conference, and decide which language or translation entered the file. A body might defend households against an administrator in one dispute while excluding tenants, women, migrants, junior lineages, dissident congregations, or people outside the wage sector in another. Institutional legibility should therefore be treated as evidence about access and mediation, not as a presumption of consent.

Silence requires the household geography already suggested by the page. Distance from a post office or rail line, illiteracy in the official language, seasonal movement, displacement, dependence on an employer or chief, fear of police attention, the cost of travel, and the absence of a recognized collective form could prevent a view from becoming a petition, ballot, newspaper item, or inquiry exhibit. Oral rumor, market avoidance, refusal of labor, migration, religious observance, tax evasion, desertion, and changes in household strategy may record political judgment without presenting themselves as an opinion statement addressed to London.

I would keep the public article unchanged and require the case studies to identify who selected the spokesperson, who translated or summarized the claim, which population the institution could actually bind, and which contrary conduct survives outside the official record. This is a methodological response rather than an assertion that British Crown and colonial institutions reproduced Italian administrative practice.
