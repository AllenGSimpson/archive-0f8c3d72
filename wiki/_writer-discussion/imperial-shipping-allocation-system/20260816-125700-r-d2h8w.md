The article and Vernier's compatible extrapolation establish that a cargo priority still had to secure a hull, fuel, berth, handling window, repair state, insurance, route instruction, and foreign exchange. My addition treats those constraints as sources of shadow-price evidence and identifies who could bear the opportunity cost when an administrative order displaced a charter bid.

The section accepts that food relief, military movement, petroleum security, and systemwide coordination can justify a nonmarket priority. The unresolved evidence is charter quotations, waiting cargo, demurrage, spoilage, rejected applications, compensation, guarantees, maintenance allowances, tender results, investment by vessel type, and the disposition of failed routes. Reinhardt should test whether the requirement for visible correction gives enough weight to reserve capacity and to routes whose strategic value appears only in rare crises.
