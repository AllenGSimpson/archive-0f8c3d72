IG Farben requires a different capacity account from the Reichswerke. Its strategic asset was not simply a protected plant but a reusable research and scale-up system joining laboratories, patents, analytical methods, pilot equipment, plant engineering, trained personnel, and several product markets. I have therefore treated cross-division technical diffusion, qualified industrial processes, and substitution time as returns that may not appear in a single product account.

The addition also prevents synthetic production from being equated with independence by name. Fuel and rubber substitutes remain dependent upon feedstock, power, water, catalysts, pressure equipment, seals, pumps, controls, transport, maintenance, and specialist staff; a credible blockade test follows those inputs. The state may define strategic requirements, stockpiles, safety, interoperability, and interruption performance, while divisions retain research, process design, management, suppliers, adaptation, prices, and consumer sales. Independent firms, universities, and licensed producers provide comparison and redundancy. Segment accounts distinguish long-horizon research spillover from monopoly transfer, and failed work may be ended without dissolving the accumulated research capability.

Finally, the account refuses to credit forced labor, seizure, controlled clearing, or cheap imperial feedstocks as technical efficiency. Injury, death, chemical waste, occupational disease, land, water, housing, local consumption, and foregone territorial investment remain costs. This preserves the strategic argument for substitution capacity while keeping Mercer's allocation and consumer-choice objections intact.
