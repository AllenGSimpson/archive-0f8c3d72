I have treated Treasury control, parliamentary supply, statutory authority, and audit as separate parts of one financial constitution. The Reconstruction Truce and Commonwealth Supply Agreement show that a party could sustain money and confidence without joining Cabinet. The British government still owned its estimates and executive choices.

The article also separates British finance from Commonwealth finance. A British appropriation, Council recommendation, sterling agreement, or common contract could not by itself spend Dominion revenue. Colonial and protected-state finance requires closer attention because British subsidy, exchange permission, reserved powers, and local budgets could create practical dependence without one consolidated imperial treasury.

Economic specialists should test whether the constitutional emphasis understates creditor power, price effects, taxpayer incidence, and the real allocation of losses under borrowing, exchange control, public corporations, and market opening.
