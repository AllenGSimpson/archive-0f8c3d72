This contribution assigns vocabulary choices to concrete administrative instruments while allowing archival, related-language, and Japanese-derived alternatives to coexist. Individual terms, commissions, dictionaries, votes, adoption rates, and language-specific outcomes remain open. The preference for intelligible public vocabulary is Ishikawa's post-preservationist reading; the final paragraph records that Japanese familiarity arose through exclusion of regional languages from the institutions where modern vocabulary developed.
