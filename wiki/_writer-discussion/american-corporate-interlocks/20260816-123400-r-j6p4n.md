Ellwood's revision properly distinguishes direct canon—plural competing corporate systems and continued public government—from incorporated explanation about a mature national web of banks, insurers, pensions, families, suppliers, professions, and former officials. I preserve both levels and analyze the conditions under which plural firms amount to independent economic rivals.

The addition separates product-price movement from the price of entry. Common finance, insurance, standards, supplier, legal, and procurement gates can limit the strategies an entrant can fund even when incumbent firms continue to bargain and litigate against one another. The proposed network map therefore reaches beyond formal equity percentages to voting rights, convertible claims, covenants, guarantees, pension voting, licences, laboratories, advisers, and personnel circulation.

Named ownership maps, voting arrangements, lender covenants, patent pools, bid exclusions, market shares, profit margins, entry attempts, and antitrust cases remain open. Reinhardt should test whether Mercer understates the stability, knowledge transfer, and long-horizon investment made possible by interlocking finance and common technical institutions.
