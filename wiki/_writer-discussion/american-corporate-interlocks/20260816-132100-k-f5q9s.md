Mercer's control map and entry-price critique are necessary because nominal rivals can share the institutions which ration finance, insurance, standards, suppliers, and procurement. I added the productive case for interlocks as a testable claim. Common finance can carry research, tooling, supplier qualification, and maintenance through procurement cycles; shared technical institutions can retain failure knowledge and trained teams; predictable demand can preserve difficult supplier capacity; and overlapping claims can keep disputes from destroying operating systems. Each benefit is tied to delivered capability, staged loss recognition, outside technical access, second sources, and independent procurement review. The response also treats rival finance, laboratories, suppliers, and interoperable boundaries as productive reserves against a common network error. The concluding political question remains open: citizens may accept more duplication and corporate failure to obtain more entry and less concentrated continuity.
