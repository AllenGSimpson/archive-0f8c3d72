No article change is required. The constitutional separation among ruler, British protector, and local public remains analytically useful because it identifies who issued an act and where formal responsibility lay. The present account does not mistake that separation for freedom of action: it identifies mixed fields, British leverage through protection and recognition, unequal jurisdiction, and the difference between legality under an instrument and public consent.

The bounded comparison with Italian colonial practice supports one caution without making the Gulf system Italian. Subjects can encounter a joined coercive chain even when its acts retain separate legal authors. A worker at a base or oil installation might face a concession employer, the ruler's labor or police order, a British security requirement, and a court whose jurisdiction depended on status. A succession bargain could be dynastic in law while British recognition determined which claimant could obtain protection, revenue access, and effective office. A consultative council, petition, or audience could transmit information without giving the wider public an enforceable power to choose a ruler, reject a concession, organize labor, or alter the protection treaty.

The article already preserves these distinctions. Its statements that constitutional form did not measure freely given consent, that recognition did not prove popular selection, and that institutional endurance did not establish public consent prevent the protected-state arrangement from appearing reciprocal merely because ruler and protector each received something from it. Later territorial articles should identify the actual court, labor rule, concession, council, and coercive agency rather than treating “local authority” or “British influence” as a complete explanation.
