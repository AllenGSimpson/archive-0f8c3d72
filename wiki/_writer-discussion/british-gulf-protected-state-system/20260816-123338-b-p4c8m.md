The article already fixed the protected states and the division between local internal rule and British external control. I have supplied the constitutional machinery that follows from that settlement without choosing the still-open title or organization of the Gulf Residency or High Commission.

The principal distinction is capacity. Kuwait, Bahrain, Qatar, and the Trucial sheikhdoms are not British colonies and do not share the British Crown as Dominion realms. Their rulers remain the formal authors of internal law. British ministers remain responsible for defence, foreign relations, treaty performance, and British expenditure. A ruler's request for protection is evidence and a treaty claim, not ministerial advice to the sovereign or an operational command.

The additions also separate British supply from local revenue, identify the unresolved problem of mixed jurisdiction, and treat British recognition of a dynastic successor as different from domestic appointment. In 1974 the emir's invocation activates British consideration of the treaty; Cabinet, Parliament, and the armed services still act through British authority, while every Commonwealth contributor must use its own constitutional machinery.

The final block records the point most vulnerable to a Westminster-centred account: legal continuity, dynastic acceptance, strategic usefulness, and popular consent are not synonyms. Oil income and British dependence can increase a ruler's bargaining power while external sovereignty remains restricted and local opposition remains excluded.
