The earlier record named wages, pensions, mobility, family service, and racial discipline but left them as a list of effects. I have recast colonial soldiering as a set of household institutions. For Eritrea, pre-divergence research supports the importance of paid service, family camps, preference for married recruits, Italian instruction, promotion, and the recruitment of veterans into the lower civil administration. These mechanisms gave households access to income and offices while making family responsibility itself an instrument of military discipline.

I have kept Somali recruitment institutionally separate. Zaptié and Dubat selection converted official ideas about noble or "martial" lineages, the recognition of elders and paid chiefs, and physical inspection into a recruitment file. Recruits and their communities still constrained this scheme: attitudes toward honorable and servile labor affected enlistment, and using soldiers for road or porter work undermined the categories through which officials sought them. This is not an Eritrean family-camp system expressed in different names.

The post-divergence paragraph follows established regional recruitment and distant deployment but does not fix a later statute. Recruitment law, dependants' allowances, pension entitlements, women's standing before military offices, and the institutional form of Ethiopian auxiliaries remain open. Future military detail should preserve the difference between payroll evidence and household experience, and should not treat service, mobility, or brokerage as consent to Crown rule.
