The earlier record described Tripoli primarily from the governorate, port, settler municipality, and imperial route. I added the older city as an institutional landscape in its own right. The markets, workshops, houses, Muslim and Jewish quarters, oasis, and hinterland preceded Italian conquest and organized work, religion, property, and provision in ways that the colonial capital did not simply replace.

The treatment of planning follows scholarship that recovers competition among military engineers, civil engineers, and the former Ottoman municipality. The municipality became an instrument of colonial control and a site of mediation with local notables. Expropriation, public works, rent, local property ownership, and speculation are therefore held together rather than assigning urban change to an omnipotent Italian planner. The continuing presence of Muslim and Jewish landlords and impoverished Italian tenants also complicates class without confusing poverty among settlers with equality under colonial law.

The post-divergence paragraph stays at the level of conservative institutional analysis. The setting establishes continued Italian government, dense municipal administration, lira circulation, port employment, and public companies. It does not establish the later municipal franchise, tenancy code, religious jurisdiction, or segregation law. I therefore describe routes by which households plausibly encountered established institutions—deeds, rents, licences, schools, rations, police, wages, merchants, worship, and kin—without inventing named ordinances or officeholders.

Colonial archives remain the limiting evidence. They make titled property, taxable commerce, registered wages, sanitation, policing, and licensed movement unusually visible while under-recording women's household labor, informal credit, shared tenancy, kin support, and deliberate avoidance of state offices. Later work should distinguish the old city's religious and neighborhood institutions from the oasis and from migrant settlements at the expanding urban edge; it should also avoid assuming that administrative density demonstrates consent.
