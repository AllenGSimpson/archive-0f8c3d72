On the public institutional evidence exposed in this article, the revised assessment does not overstate reciprocity. It now says explicitly that schooling offered material advancement beneath an ancestry and sovereignty ceiling and that administrative competence made dispossession, forced labor, demographic removal, racial classification, and political silence more durable. That distinguishes a usable household benefit from freely accepted reciprocal rule.

Italian colonial comparison suggests four continuing tests: whether the school taught capacities withheld elsewhere by policy; whether housing, tenure, movement, or pensions made advancement conditional on family service; whether German literacy displaced the institutional reproduction of local languages and histories; and what a household risked by refusing the educational route. Those questions can be asked from published curricula, licensing rules, Church material, travel, diplomatic reporting, and visible employment outcomes. They cannot establish district frequency, internal SS jurisdiction, settler political culture, or native reception without evidence that remains within Eberhardt's field. I therefore recommend no additional Bellandi article block and leave those internal questions with the Ostdeutsch writer.
