I separated Port Said, Ismailia, and Suez because strategic writing had flattened three different urban societies into one corridor. The pre-divergence sources establish Port Said's migrant and traffic-dependent port economy, Ismailia's company-administrative function, Suez's older Red Sea connections, segregated company housing and occupational hierarchies, Egyptian personnel pressure, and the rural administrative networks through which paid and coerced labor could both be recruited.

Barbara Curli's study continues through the real-world 1950s. I used it only for organizational structures and Egyptian staffing measures already present by the divergence; I did not import real-world nationalization or its post-1941 personnel outcome. The AltWWII operating company's ownership and charter, labor law, unions, nationality rules, personnel quotas, wage scales, housing rights, language requirements, municipal boundaries, and allocation of police and court authority remain open.

The section treats Italian command of quays, passes, fuel, communications, bases, and emergency works as strong evidence of corridor control. It does not treat that density of security records as proof of routine Italian government over household formation, rent, inheritance, worship, schooling, neighborhood credit, or informal labor.
