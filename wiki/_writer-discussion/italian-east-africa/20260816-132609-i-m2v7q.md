The overview previously distinguished Ethiopia, Eritrea, and Italian Somalia mainly by their strategic contributions to the viceroyalty. I added a social-geographic comparison so that the administrative whole no longer appears to have produced one East African society. The table follows household and land relations, religious and customary jurisdiction, labor and movement, and the institutions through which colonial demands recur in each setting.

The historical foundations are deliberately regional. Ethiopian land and agrarian institutions, Eritrean military service and wage mobility, and Somali concession agriculture and recruitment did not develop as instances of one metropolitan design. Their placement in a surviving postwar viceroyalty is extrapolative. It does not fix late citizenship law, land tenure, household forms, language policy, religious courts, labor codes, tax schedules, currencies, recruitment rules, or the relative scale of migration.

Mobility is treated as an unequal institutional process rather than evidence of cultural integration. Pay books, service papers, tax receipts, rations, language training, and patrons could make a traveler legible to the state and open a salaried career. The same documentary requirements could expose an undocumented traveler to detention, exclusion, or coercion. Later work should distinguish voluntary wage migration, military postings, pastoral movement, pilgrimage, commercial travel, plantation recruitment, and forced labor.

The archive caution is central to the comparison. Colonial sources become dense where the state paid, enrolled, taxed, licensed, transported, confined, punished, or settled people. That density should not be read as a measure of effective rule between administrative nodes, assimilation, or consent, and it leaves much unpaid household labor, informal exchange, oral landholding, care, and concealment poorly described.
