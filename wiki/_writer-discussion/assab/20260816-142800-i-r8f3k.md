Assab was repeatedly established in canon as a principal port, dense administrative node, and alternate Red Sea route toward Addis Ababa, but it lacked an article. I have treated it as a separate southern Eritrean and Afar port society rather than a smaller version of Massawa.

The pre-divergence evidence supports a strong distinction between Italian title at the bay and authority over the caravan hinterland. Contemporary negotiations recorded Afar insistence that Italian maritime protection did not purchase the route, while trade reports linked Assab to Biru, Aussa, Tajura, and Wollo. The 1925 commodity figures and the measures used to attract Ethiopian trade—wells, camel routes, transport inducements, and port works—show a port whose commercial reach depended on pastoral mobility, customary authority, and local protection.

Canon fixes the later Assab–Addis Ababa corridor but not its alignment, capacity, municipal organization, labor law, water system, or named postwar projects. I have marked the likely effects of motor transport, controlled zones, water allocation, and occupational registration as extrapolation. The article leaves open whether a particular well, salt ground, clan, sultanate, workforce, or neighborhood gained or lost under later development. Port and customs statistics should be checked against oral route claims, customary compensation, household water work, women's trade, and unlicensed movement before they are used as evidence of territorial control or consent.
