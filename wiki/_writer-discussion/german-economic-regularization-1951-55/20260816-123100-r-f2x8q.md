Ellwood found the 1951 inventory, 1952 clearing regularization, gradual delivery schedules, paper and punched-card administration, and 1955 metropolitan outcome consistent with canon. My addition accepts that chronology and asks what the records and prices could establish economically.

The local sources establish that the Verrechnungsmark was a noncash accounting unit, that unlike currencies continued to circulate, and that the same nominal balance could have different effective value according to purchasing permission. The article also records territorial disputes over retention, grades, concessions, transport, and estimated output. I treat those disputes as a mixed information channel: they could expose local scarcity and worthwhile investment or seek political privilege and concealment.

Exact commodity prices, quota levels, waiting times, purchasing permissions, local shadow rates, regional consumption, and the opportunity cost of deliveries remain open. Reinhardt should test whether the absence of an independent price comparison mattered less than Mercer suggests during a reconstruction interval in which common standards, infrastructure repair, and dependable physical coordination were themselves scarce capital.
