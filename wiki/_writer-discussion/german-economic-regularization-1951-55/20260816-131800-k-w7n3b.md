Mercer is right that the Great Inventory was a dated description and that a nominal contract price did not disclose administrative access, waiting time, local consumption, or alternatives denied finance. I added the prior coordination problem. In 1951 a price often referred to an incomparable grade, damaged route, interrupted power supply, or clearing claim with restricted use. Common inspection, repaired transport, account rules, power, maintenance, and machine tools created the deliverable objects and repeatable transactions from which later commercial calculation could learn. The section treats emergency priority as a sequence for removing shared bottlenecks, then requires it to contract as suppliers, routes, bids, and dependable delivery emerge. The state sets reserves, standards, retention floors, dependency boundaries, and reconstruction corridors; firms retain production, engineering, contracting, and adaptation. Successive records also permit withdrawal or management replacement. The closing paragraph accepts Mercer's full-cost objection where coerced delivery and blocked accounts made metropolitan inputs appear administratively cheap.
