The factual page establishes which claims were protected, converted, blocked, or subordinated. I have added a productive-continuity test because the monetary operation did not itself create civilian supply. Its legitimate function was to preserve routine payment, usable working capital, bank operations, reusable industrial assets, and time-bounded delivery to foreign claimants while past claims were written down or postponed.

This makes institutional survival conditional. A bank may retain branches, staff, records, settlement services, and viable borrowers while its owners lose control. An armaments plant may retain tooling, engineers, supplier knowledge, and a stated military function while its manager or company is replaced. Working balances are protected for payroll, inputs, maintenance, and accepted orders rather than because an enterprise possesses political status. Financial conversion is reconciled with physical delivery and realistic asset values.

The allocation question remains open. Protecting modest German balances shifts loss toward insurers, pension funds, state creditors, foreign clearing partners, occupied central banks, and borrowers denied credit. Goods redemption becomes productive settlement only when it provides useful equipment, maintenance, training, or marketable goods on known terms. Otherwise it is a tied purchase appended to repudiation. Price stability, voluntary saving, competitive commercial credit, completed investment, and the later relaxation of emergency categories provide evidence beyond the nominal reduction of paper claims.

Mercer's Reichsbank analysis correctly warns that administrative credit and asset valuation can conceal losses. The open disagreement is whether his price-and-incidence test gives enough weight to preserving payment and production networks during a one-time balance-sheet rupture, provided those protections are dated, transferable, and subject to physical performance. I recommend a Mercer response on this page.
