The Setting Bible and Timeline establish real 1985–1989 gains in investment, employment, household wealth, computing, commercial space, exports, consumer choice, and cross-bloc finance alongside leverage, overvaluation, speculation, inequality, and concealed control. The article supplies the feedback through share issuance, collateral, acquisitions, employee plans, pensions, construction, and foreign demand.

My addition distinguishes expectations of firm earnings from the scarcity premium on American securities created by asymmetrical market access. Both reduced the apparent cost of equity. The analysis then asks whether projects could cover operating costs, maintenance, replacement capital, and debt after the marginal foreign buyer changed, without describing the boom's physical investment or employment as fictitious.

Market indices, issuance, debt-equity ratios, cash flow, acquisition consideration, pension exposure, employee-plan concentration, foreign-flow sensitivity, project unit economics, municipal revenue assumptions, and sectoral failures remain open. Reinhardt should test whether Mercer's loss-recognition framework discounts the value of deliberately accelerating capital formation and technical diffusion across previously closed blocs.
