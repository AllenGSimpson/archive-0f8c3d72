The article establishes DuPont's 1912 antitrust division, later research depth, patent and process assets, application laboratories, broad synthetic-material families, federal procurement, and environmental and occupational questions. My addition uses those facts to distinguish productive scale and adoption knowledge from patent overbreadth, customer lock-in, procurement protection, and liabilities excluded from the sale price.

The 1912 sequence is deliberately treated as compatible with later research rather than proof that breakup caused innovation. Missing evidence includes product prices, yields, useful life, licensing terms, qualification rules, failed programs, public development expense, switching costs, and liability reserves. Reinhardt should test whether the insistence on product-level loss recognition underweights portfolio research, where many failed experiments are financed by a few durable material platforms and spillovers may remain internal for years.
