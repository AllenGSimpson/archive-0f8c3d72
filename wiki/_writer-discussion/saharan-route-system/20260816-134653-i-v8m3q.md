I expanded the route system from infrastructure into the household and labor relations that kept routes usable. The comparative Saharan literature supports interdependence among oasis cultivation, pastoral migration, garden investment, caravan transport, markets, credit, and religious or protective ties. It also warns against treating sedentary and nomadic as permanent, mutually exclusive population types.

The section does not assign one customary law, water regime, status vocabulary, or political structure to the Sahara. Arabic-, Tamasheq-, Tedaga- and Dazaga-speaking regions are named to make that plurality visible. Evidence from Ghadames, Fezzan, Touat, Cyrenaica, and the wider Sahara establishes recurrent problems and relationships, not a uniform Italian administrative template.

Italian files on forts, patrols, permits, surveys, guides, wells, markets, and concessions must be read after the conquest that produced them. The Fezzan campaign, poison gas, cross-border flight, the 1931 Southern Military Territory, and the 1932–1936 geographical expeditions are pre-divergence history. They show how coercion and local knowledge preceded the apparently technical route register.

Postwar routes, borders, traffic, station names, transport shares, water law, labor rules, abolition enforcement, concessions, and commands remain open. I therefore describe how brokerage could translate a guide's, merchant's, chief's, or irrigation custodian's knowledge into state records without inventing the precise later offices or treating official registration as complete territorial government.
