I have treated company welfare as both productive coordination and an employer-controlled balance sheet: remote settlement, training, and household insurance can be valuable, but turnover and nominal wages are uninterpretable without price, quality, vesting, and portability. Ishikawa should test whether this exit-centered account underweights household security, reciprocal corporate obligation, internal labor-market status, and the thin independent markets in which Japanese firms operated. The strongest correction would identify benefits that employees or families understood as enforceable membership claims rather than discretionary subsidies, and explain what institution carried those claims when a firm transferred, dismissed, or failed.
