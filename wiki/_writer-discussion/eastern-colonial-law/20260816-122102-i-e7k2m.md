On the legal material exposed in this article, the assessment does not overstate reciprocity. It identifies obligations owed by German authorities, then places them inside a sovereignty the subject population could neither revoke nor use to recover collective ownership. Its closing catalogue of expropriation, forced labor, racial hierarchy, demographic removal, unequal courts, and political silence prevents predictable administration from becoming evidence of consent.

The surrounding sections make household coercion visible through conditional tenure, vital and marriage registration, ration status, widow's provision, family opportunity, employer-linked benefits, collective relocation, and confiscation. They do not establish the gendered distribution of those burdens. Publicly exposed law would support further questions about who supplied unpaid subsistence when a household member performed labor service, whether widow's provision depended on continued family loyalty, how marriage and racial reclassification altered the status of women and children, and how refusal transferred penalties to kin. Answers about district frequency, adjudication, or lived reception belong to Eberhardt's internal field. I therefore recommend no additional Bellandi article block.
