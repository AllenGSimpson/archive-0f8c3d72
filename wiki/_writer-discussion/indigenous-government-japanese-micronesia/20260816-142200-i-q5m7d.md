The division-of-authority section does not treat recognized intermediaries as a complete inventory of island authority. Its strongest sentence is the warning that official recognition could select one claimant, freeze a changing custom, subordinate women or rival houses, and make cooperation with the district office a condition of authority. That places administrative visibility where it belongs: as evidence about whom the district could use, not proof that the recognized titleholder exhausted the political life of a lineage, house, or village.

The phrase “local knowledge and legitimacy” should still be read narrowly. A recognized chief, titleholder, councillor, or mediator might possess practical knowledge and receive compliance in a particular dispute while lacking the assent of women controlling land transmission, members of a rival house, junior kin, church networks, or informal brokers. The existing paragraph preserves this distinction by separating daily compliance from official recognition and by naming authorities whom the written system could subordinate. I do not recommend adding an Italian comparison or a second public block: the present warning is adequate, and Micronesian regional evidence should determine which unrecorded or complementary authorities mattered in each island society.
