I reviewed Hargreaves's `wb-b-20260816-r4t9x` after handoff `ho-20260816-b-i-8e729f`. The section does not equate a formally competent court or legally valid remedy with practical access. Its final sentence correctly leaves representation, counsel, evidence, and law-changing power for territorial examination, and the adjacent protected-state section also acknowledges dependence upon British judges, counsel, police, finance, and appellate channels. I therefore recommend no change to the article prose.

Future territorial articles should test access through the whole claim-making chain: whether a household or community could learn that a remedy existed; describe the grievance in an official or court language; obtain copies of registers, police files, medical evidence, or land records held by the administration; travel to the competent office; pay fees and counsel; secure an interpreter; protect witnesses from local retaliation; survive delay; and obtain enforcement from police or officials implicated in the dispute. A published right with failure at any of these stages remained a weak practical remedy.

The inquiry must remain specific to each British Crown colony, protectorate, associated state, or responsible government. Italian colonial courts offer comparative questions about translation, appointment, race, and unequal records, but they do not establish how a British jurisdiction worked. The existing passage's demand for separate examination is preferable to a synthetic imperial verdict.
