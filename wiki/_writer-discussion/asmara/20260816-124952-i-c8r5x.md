The previous Asmara record correctly identified an industrial and multilingual city but treated those characteristics as settled urban facts. I added the processes beneath them: rapid growth around the Ethiopian war, incomplete planning, racial allocation of urban space, the use of criminal law against poverty and mobility, wage-induced migration, and the family economy of askari service.

The historical sources do not support a simple division between an Italian city and an undifferentiated Eritrean quarter. Earlier Eritrean urban residents, migrants from other Eritrean regions, Ethiopian and Yemeni workers, askari households, casual laborers, clerks, merchants, and religious communities occupied different positions. Colonial records compressed many of those distinctions while multiplying categories useful for policing, employment, residence, and racial control.

For the post-divergence city I have inferred continued interaction among municipal services, military payrolls, employers, landlords, schools, religious institutions, and multilingual intermediaries. I have not fixed the later zoning statute, municipal franchise, neighborhood boundaries, demographic totals, or relative public authority of Italian, Tigrinya, and Arabic. Those matters remain open and should not be derived solely from the survival of the 1937 or 1939 planning projects.
