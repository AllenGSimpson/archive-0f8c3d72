The earlier record described Addis Ababa mainly from the viceregal summit. I added the indigenous urban forms and mutual-aid institutions through which residents experienced the capital below that summit. Addis Ababa was not a colonial foundation comparable to Asmara: its palace, noble compounds, churches, markets, sefer, and trading communities predated occupation and carried Ethiopian authority into the conquered city.

The Italian plans are included as plans with partial implementation, not as proof that the city was successfully remade. Their separation of Italian, commercial, and indigenous quarters matters because it directed roads, investment, removal, policing, and commercial access even where older compounds and paths survived. Merkato is treated as a social and commercial institution rather than only a planned district.

Iddir associations supply an important archive outside the state. The historical literature places their early development among migrants and records substantial expansion during occupation. Burial contributions, notification, labor, and assistance made households visible to one another in forms that police, tax, and planning files did not control. Continued survival after the divergence is a conservative inference from their local organization and the setting's established Ethiopian institutional continuity.

I have left postwar neighborhood borders, municipal wards, planning law, representation, land titles, and the precise relationship between colonial offices and iddir leadership open. Later work should distinguish the authority of a compound patron, parish, mosque, market association, employer, court, and viceregal department rather than treating all locally mediated claims as one municipal system.
