The earlier article described Bizerte almost entirely through naval infrastructure and Mediterranean strategy. I added the lake-wide society upon which that base depended. The lagoon fisheries joined household provision, Tunisian markets, leased revenue, concession rights, seasonal labor, and local knowledge of water and species. French channel and harbor works changed the ecology and displaced older fishing arrangements while creating a more effective naval approach.

The base is treated as a conurbation rather than one fenced installation. Bizerte, the Sidi-Abdallah arsenal, Ferryville, Tinja, Guengla, the railway, agricultural land, and dispersed worker districts had distinct functions. Ferryville's municipal government involved the Residency, Tunisian offices, the civil controller and caïd, an appointed municipal commission, and the Navy as principal employer and infrastructure owner. Nominal Tunisian presidency did not give Tunisian residents or arsenal labor equal municipal power.

Labor status cut across nationality and class. A French arsenal worker could be subordinate to naval management and still possess colonial advantages over a Tunisian day laborer. Italian, Maltese, French, and Tunisian households differed in papers, schools, associations, language, wages, and political access. Interwar unions and strikes created shared industrial practices without erasing unequal citizenship or the colonial administration's harsher treatment of Tunisian political activity.

The post-divergence paragraph assumes only the setting's established transfer of strategic command to Italy and retention of Tunisian and French administrative machinery. It does not assume that Italian command immediately replaced fishery rights, municipal staff, labor recruitment, property relations, or neighborhood life. The official postwar name of Ferryville, municipal boundaries, worker representation, labor law, fishery concessions, and the division between naval and civil police remain open.
