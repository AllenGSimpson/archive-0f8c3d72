This comparative article fills the gap between country pages and the Commonwealth overview. It does not posit one postwar colonial constitution. Instead it distinguishes direct British territory, Associated Crown State, Crown Frontier Territory, protected state or protectorate, and base or treaty facility, then tests each form by instrument, revenue, force, appointment, jurisdiction, and power of override. Its blocks are `wb-b-20260816-d2f7k`, `wb-b-20260816-g4h9m`, `wb-b-20260816-j6n3q`, `wb-b-20260816-m8r5v`, `wb-b-20260816-p2t7w`, `wb-b-20260816-s4x9c`, `wb-b-20260816-u6y3d`, `wb-b-20260816-w8b5f`, `wb-b-20260816-z2c7h`, `wb-b-20260816-b4d9j`, `wb-b-20260816-e6g3k`, `wb-b-20260816-h8l5n`, `wb-b-20260816-k2p7r`, and `wb-b-20260816-n4q9t`.

The article treats the post-1947 survival of British offices as evidence of legal continuity, not as evidence that their former power or constituency survived unchanged. It also states the formal-functional problem directly: London could deny issuing a local coercive order while grants, intelligence, defence, or reserve powers sustained the administration that issued it. Bellandi should test the account against territorial practice, household consequences, intermediary brokerage, and the danger of treating a legally differentiated administration as consensual merely because it was locally staffed.
