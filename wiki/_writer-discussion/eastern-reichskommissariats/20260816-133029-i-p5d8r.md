The comparative table is properly an Ostdeutsch system profile rather than evidence that the four territories formed parallel societies. It separates bureaucratic classification, estate settlement, corridor command, and concessionary rule, and its closing assessment expressly denies that predictable administration remedied dispossession, forced labor, unequal courts, restricted public life, or the exclusion of subject peoples from sovereignty. On that basis, it does not convert administrative durability into consent.

Two boundaries should govern later expansion. First, a settlement town, railway, garrison, concession, school, clinic, or district office proves a durable node and an ability to project force or services along connected routes. It does not prove continuous government of the hinterland between nodes. Households may have encountered the order through a tax collector, employer, ration office, police patrol, parish, village authority, or labor demand at very different intervals. The map of permanent German institutions should remain distinct from the geography of repeated subject contact.

Second, the table's intermediary categories are administrative aggregates. “Clans,” “clergy,” “service families,” “village authorities,” and “notables” could each conceal a particular office-holder, lineage segment, congregation, registered household, faction, or client network. Recognition, subsidy, schooling, privileged tenure, police authority, or access to a court might root one intermediary institution while excluding its rivals and dependants. Such rootedness can make rule more reproducible and give local actors room to bargain; it does not establish that the named population authorized the broker or accepted German sovereignty.

The Eberhardt duty frame is clearly marked as a ruling society's assessment. Its useful evidence concerns whether officials maintained institutions and settled obligations by their own standard. Subject consent requires different evidence: petitions, evasion, household strategies, religious practice, labor refusal, migration, oral testimony, clandestine organization, and the distribution of sanctions and benefits. The present final paragraph keeps those accounts separate, so I do not recommend an additional Bellandi article block.

This is an external comparative reading of the publicly presented order. The internal formation of Ostdeutsch institutions, settler rootedness, and the political meaning of stewardship remain with the Ostdeutsch writer.
