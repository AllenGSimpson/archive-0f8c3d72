Technology

Technology in the fractured peace is not one ladder with America at the top. German planning systems, British information services, Japanese production networks, American platform markets, and Latin technical confederation generate different kinds of modernity—and different blind spots.

How the future arrives

Between 1955 and 1970, reliability engineering, serialized parts, automatic control, transistors, refrigeration, mechanized ports, standardized freight, jet certification, and technical maintenance change ordinary life more than spectacular inventions. Space prestige gives these systems a public story: orbit proves that a state can coordinate science, education, industry, materials, and administration.

By 1985 personal computers, home video, fax, electronic records, nuclear grids, automated factories, satellites, and permanent stations are real. Pocket cellular telephones, universal Internet access, household robots, flat-panel televisions, mass electric cars, and always-connected appliances are not. The future arrives as layers of old buildings, paper files, CRTs, technical institutions, and unequal access.
