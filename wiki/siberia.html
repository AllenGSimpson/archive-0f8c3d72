Russian and Siberian Successor States

No single Russia succeeds the Soviet Union after the collapse of 1945–46. The Ural settlement of 30 June 1946 and the organized eastern evacuation disperse factories, military districts, party committees, refugee administrations, security organs, and railway systems among regional successor bureaucracies with armies. They retain Soviet forms while growing increasingly Russian-national, local, and independent.[a]

The Soviet ark breaks apart

The Soviet east is not an empty wilderness. Wartime evacuation has already moved factories, engineers, administrators, soldiers, camps, food systems, and millions of civilians toward the Urals, Siberia, and Central Asia. After Stalin is removed, the collective survival leadership of Malenkov, Molotov, Beria, Zhukov, and Khrushchev obtains a 1946 settlement permitting a second great movement of current populations, factories, machines, rolling stock, archives, stores, and formations from the remaining western territory.[a]

The resulting fragmentation is institutional rather than instant village-level warlordism. The crowded industrial refuges preserve more design teams than the successor governments can finance, beginning the second migration of engineers and archives into India. German deportations later add urban families, surplus peasants, officials, soldiers, collaborators, prisoners, and people cleared from settlement corridors. German-occupied Russia remains populated because the empire still needs miners, railway workers, farm laborers, technical specialists, servants, and auxiliaries.[a]

This is not instant village-level warlordism. The “warlords” call themselves marshals, chairmen, commissars, governors, and emergency defense councils. Their authority grows from ministries, factories, barracks, prisons, railways, and ration systems rather than personal cavalry bands.[a]

The resulting Siberian Wars are usually fought by hundreds rather than world-war armies. Their cumulative effects—broken railways, expelled populations, fortified borders, and repeated family migration—are nevertheless continental.[a]

Kazakhstan and Central Asia

Kazakhstan becomes the great compression chamber. Northern cities, rail corridors, mines, and military settlements acquire large Russian populations, while much of the countryside remains Kazakh. Refugees arrive as victims of German conquest and agents of continued requisition, settlement, and administrative domination at the same time.[a]

The Kazakh–Russian War of 1954–1963 turns this conflict into the largest post-Soviet population war. Mass expulsion and a rough front-line partition leave a Kazakh south and a Russian-held north organized as the Russian Steppe State.[a]

The Central Asian successor states remain a separate southern system rather than an extension of the Siberian governments. Tashkent is a major administrative, intellectual, railway, and refugee capital. Its Administrative Union preserves a multinational claim to Soviet continuity while becoming increasingly Uzbek-centered.[a]

Survival between two empires

Germany blocks the west and Japan constrains the Pacific. The interior possesses minerals, factories, rivers, camps, and trained people but lacks reliable access to world trade. Successor states depend on rail corridors, smuggling, Central Asian routes, Iran where accessible, and American Arctic “humanitarian” aid that mixes food and medicine with radios, machinery, advisers, and intelligence.[a]

The Siberian black market becomes the practical circulatory system of the region. Railway officials, pilots, customs officers, brokers, and intelligence services move bearings, fuel, radio parts, antibiotics, ammunition, machine tools, and documents across frontiers their governments publicly enforce.[e]

Neither Germany nor Japan wants a revived strong Russia. Each can tolerate weak buffers and clients while disrupting any state capable of crossing the Urals or recovering the Pacific coast.[a]

Arctic aid and river transport

American Arctic aid reaches the interior as a thin seasonal lifeline rather than a replacement for the lost Soviet supply economy. Atlantic traffic approaches the Kara Sea while Pacific traffic passes through the Bering and eastern Arctic seas under Japanese scrutiny. Food, medicine, seed, machinery, radios, engines, cranes, shallow-draft craft, and technical personnel form the characteristic cargo.[a]

The Northern Sea Route and river fleets survive as divided systems of pilots, ice reports, ports, barges, repair yards, and local agreements. Summer unloading, upriver movement, autumn distribution, and winter repair define the annual logistical calendar.[e]

Russia beyond the Urals

Soviet institutions remain the language people know, but Marxist universalism loses force after the loss of European Russia. Red Army commanders become Russian first and Communist second. Orthodox practice returns unevenly beneath Soviet administrative habits. Refugee memory, betrayal, endurance, and reconquest replace world revolution.[a]

The emerging identity is militarized, refugee-heavy, anti-German, hostile to Japan, suspicious of Central Asian autonomy, and resentful of powers that did not save it. Its political language draws from Soviet administration, Russian nationalism, Orthodoxy, and refugee memory. It is commonly described as Russia beyond the Urals.[a]

Cities and the railway state system

The Trans-Siberian Railway does not reunify the Soviet east. It creates a chain of governments able to tax, interrupt, or protect traffic at river crossings and yards. Omsk supplies military administration, while Novosibirsk supplies industrial and technical authority. Krasnoyarsk controls the Yenisei crossing and Arctic transfers. Irkutsk guards the Baikal frontier and break of gauge.[a]

Farther west, Nizhny Tagil, Chelyabinsk, and Magnitogorsk preserve separate pieces of the Soviet armor and metallurgical system. Their factories require one another while their emergency governments resist political consolidation.[e]

The Tehran principle

The December 1985 Tehran Concord recognizes a German restraint line along the Siberian slopes of the Urals and a Japanese line along the western shore of Lake Baikal to the Arctic. Siberian and Central Asian successor states in the intervening belt are awarded to neither sphere. Sphere recognition governs German and Japanese restraint toward one another; it does not erase local sovereignty.[a]

The settlement reduces interstate rivalry without resolving Russian statehood or creating a German–Japanese alliance. Trade, concessions, intelligence, aid, and client relationships continue across a frontier whose governments still dispute who, if anyone, represents Russia.[a]
