Russian and Siberian Successor States

No single Russia succeeds the Soviet Union after the collapse of 1945–46. The Ural settlement and organized eastern evacuation disperse factories, military districts, party committees, refugee administrations, security organs, and railway systems among regional successor bureaucracies with armies. They retain Soviet forms while growing increasingly Russian-national, local, and independent.

The Soviet ark breaks apart

The Soviet east is not an empty wilderness. Wartime evacuation has already moved factories, engineers, administrators, soldiers, camps, food systems, and millions of civilians toward the Urals, Siberia, and Central Asia. After Stalin is removed, the collective survival leadership of Malenkov, Molotov, Beria, Zhukov, and Khrushchev obtains a 1946 settlement permitting a second great movement of current populations, factories, machines, rolling stock, archives, stores, and formations from the remaining western territory.

The resulting fragmentation is institutional rather than instant village-level warlordism. The crowded industrial refuges preserve more design teams than the successor governments can finance, beginning the second migration of engineers and archives into India. German deportations later add urban families, surplus peasants, officials, soldiers, collaborators, prisoners, and people cleared from settlement corridors. German-occupied Russia remains populated because the empire still needs miners, railway workers, farm laborers, technical specialists, servants, and auxiliaries.

This is not instant village-level warlordism. The “warlords” call themselves marshals, chairmen, commissars, governors, and emergency defense councils. Their authority grows from ministries, factories, barracks, prisons, railways, and ration systems rather than personal cavalry bands.

The resulting Siberian Wars are usually fought by hundreds rather than world-war armies. Their cumulative effects—broken railways, expelled populations, fortified borders, and repeated family migration—are nevertheless continental.

Kazakhstan and Central Asia

Kazakhstan becomes the great compression chamber. Northern cities, rail corridors, mines, and military settlements acquire large Russian populations, while much of the countryside remains Kazakh. Refugees arrive as victims of German conquest and agents of continued requisition, settlement, and administrative domination at the same time.

The Kazakh–Russian War of 1954–1963 turns this conflict into the largest post-Soviet population war. Mass expulsion and a rough front-line partition leave a Kazakh south and a Russian-held north organized as the Russian Steppe State. Tashkent remains an administrative and intellectual refuge while surrounding cities fill with soldiers, technicians, evacuees, and black markets.

Survival between two empires

Germany blocks the west and Japan constrains the Pacific. The interior possesses minerals, factories, rivers, camps, and trained people but lacks reliable access to world trade. Successor states depend on rail corridors, smuggling, Central Asian routes, Iran where accessible, and American Arctic “humanitarian” aid that mixes food and medicine with radios, machinery, advisers, and intelligence.

The Siberian black market becomes the practical circulatory system of the region. Railway officials, pilots, customs officers, brokers, and intelligence services move bearings, fuel, radio parts, antibiotics, ammunition, machine tools, and documents across frontiers their governments publicly enforce.

Neither Germany nor Japan wants a revived strong Russia. Each can tolerate weak buffers and clients while disrupting any state capable of crossing the Urals or recovering the Pacific coast.

Russia beyond the Urals

Soviet institutions remain the language people know, but Marxist universalism loses force after the loss of European Russia. Red Army commanders become Russian first and Communist second. Orthodox practice returns unevenly beneath Soviet administrative habits. Refugee memory, betrayal, endurance, and reconquest replace world revolution.

The emerging identity is militarized, refugee-heavy, anti-German, hostile to Japan, suspicious of Central Asian autonomy, and resentful of powers that did not save it. It is not liberal exile, restored Tsarism, or unchanged Stalinism. It is Russia beyond the Urals.

The Tehran principle

The 1985 Tehran Concord draws German and Japanese restraint lines but does not divide every frontier government between them. Central Asian states between the Siberian Urals and Japan's Lake Baikal line belong to neither sphere. Sphere recognition constrains Berlin and Tokyo; it does not erase local sovereignty.

The settlement therefore freezes competition rather than resolving Russian statehood. Trade, concessions, intelligence, aid, and client relationships continue across a frontier whose governments still dispute who, if anyone, represents Russia.
