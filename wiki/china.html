National Government of China

The National Government of the Republic of China is a formally unified Nanjing-based state descended from the wartime Reorganized Government. In practice it is a weak confederation of provincial authorities operating under Japanese guardianship: stable enough to feed, police, and administer itself, but deliberately denied the industrial and military capacities of full sovereignty.

Formation

Japan formally recognizes the Nanjing government as the government of China in 1947–48. Stronger campaigns around Chongqing and Ichi-Go have isolated the old resistance, while the decade after the war is spent destroying major Communist bases and reducing armed movements to local unrest, banditry, and underground networks.

Japan preserves one Chinese flag and diplomatic voice because a legible center is easier to manage than permanent fragmentation. The provinces nevertheless govern like semi-states. Japanese advisers decide strategic questions; provincial forces police roads, railways, mines, and towns with obsolete Japanese equipment.

Territorial settlement

Nanjing's recognition as the government of China does not restore the full Qing frontier. Manchukuo, the United Mongolian State, Xinjiang, and Tibet are separate sovereign or protected states within Japan's strategic ring. The National Government neither claims nor administers Xinjiang or Tibet.

The settlement makes the republic's Han-centered identity more coherent while preventing it from acquiring the frontier depth and independent military system of a continental great power. Official culture presents the surrounding states as a protective wall built around Chinese civilization rather than as territories lost to Japanese policy.

A managed civilization

The government of National China is a Five-Yuan Republican superstructure resting upon a provincial confederation. Nanjing speaks for the nation, but its authority is negotiated through provincial officers, landlords, merchants, magistrates, clans, and Japanese missions. Tianjin and Wuhan are heavily watched transport-industrial centers. Guangzhou remains exposed to smuggling and overseas networks. Chongqing and Sichuan preserve resistance memory. Shanxi and Shaanxi are scarred pacification zones.

By the 1960s China is quieter without being loyal. After war from 1937 through 1947 and another decade of campaigns, ordinary people know that rebellion brings requisition, reprisal, famine, and death. The regime's stability is therefore rooted as much in exhaustion as in legitimacy.

Security and the provincial armies

Chinese troops receive enough organization, radios, vehicles, and old armor to suppress rebellion and protect extraction corridors, but not enough independent command, modern weapons, or national logistics to challenge Japan. Staff colleges teach railway protection, village isolation, anti-bandit intelligence, and coordination with Japanese advisers.

This creates the client army's central contradiction. The more competent it becomes at pacifying China, the more its officers can imagine defending China. A generation trained in order rather than revolution may eventually redirect the state's own institutions toward national sovereignty.

The industrial ceiling

The Chinese economy remains agrarian-heavy and extractive-heavy. Provinces may build farm tools, textiles, food processing, fertilizer, cement, rail repair shops, simple ammunition, police equipment, and basic machine shops. Irrigation, roads, ports, warehouses, mines, and communications are modernized when they improve output, taxation, policing, or export.

Automobiles, aircraft, advanced chemicals, heavy machine tools, modern armored vehicles, independent steel expansion, national arms production, strategic electronics, and major shipbuilding are restricted or placed under Japanese ownership and supervision. Manchukuo's heavy industry proves that Chinese land can industrialize—but only when severed from Chinese sovereignty and placed under Japanese command.

Enough industry for dependence to be efficient; not enough industry for independence to become possible.

The restored examination order

The early-1950s National Examination Settlement restores a recognizable gentry ladder through classical-administrative, practical-service, and technical-professional tracks. Classics, moral government, tax registers, public health, engineering, and Japanese technical reading lead into different parts of the public order.

The system gives families a path to status and binds literate elites to Nanjing. It also creates a divide between the classical national elite and the bilingual technical elite described in the language order: society honors literary cultivation while Japanese industry rewards scarce mathematical and engineering skill.

Culture, media, and regional society

National culture is a Republican appropriation of imperial civilization rather than a dynastic restoration. Sun Yat-sen, Confucian public ethics, traditional characters, historical ceremony, and restored lineage institutions coexist with modern clothing, Japanese appliances, and a powerful Chinese media world of historical drama, opera, wuxia, publishing, and provincial broadcasting.

The state is internally plural. Nanjing, Shanghai, northern China, Sichuan, and Guangdong possess different political economies and memories. The shared claim that China is Asia's civilizational center is sustained by Sino-Japanese guardianship, under which Japan retains the machinery of defense and high industry.

Technocratic nationalist restoration

Japan's deepest fear is not immediate revolt but successful Chinese recovery. The desired balance—unified yet divided, stable yet weak, modern enough to serve yet too traditional to challenge—cannot be held indefinitely.

By the later 1980s the most plausible opposition is conservative, industrial, and status-seeking rather than Maoist. The working National Learning and Practical State movement joins classical purpose to demands for Chinese machine tools, management, credentials, and technical sovereignty.
