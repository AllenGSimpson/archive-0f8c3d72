Kuwait War and Operation Covenant

The Kuwait War was Britain's first unambiguous military victory since its 1947 defeat. German-equipped National Socialist Iraq invaded the British-protected province. Commonwealth forces answered with Operation Covenant and demonstrated that hardware unsupported by institutions did not make a modern army.

Iraq and the Gulf

After civil war, a German-backed Arab National Revolutionary Command ruled Iraq, suppressed rivals, and claimed leadership of the Arab nation. The British-protected province or emirate of Kuwait was treated in Baghdad as territory severed from Basra rather than an independent Kuwaiti nation.

Britain could not defend every old Middle Eastern obligation, but Kuwait's oil, bases, Gulf access, and treaty credibility made abandonment strategically ruinous. Saudi Arabia, Iran, Jordan, and the Latin bloc all feared Iraqi expansion for different reasons.

Operation al-ʿAwda

In September 1974 Iraq invaded under Operation al-ʿAwda, presenting the campaign as national restoration. Its forces possessed advanced German armor, aircraft, missiles, and communications. They lacked the German maintenance culture, staff work, noncommissioned officer corps, logistics, and combined-arms competence required to use them as a system.

The invasion was dangerous but brittle. Iraqi formations outran support and struggled to coordinate air defense, maneuver, repair, and supply once Commonwealth interdiction began.

Operation Covenant

Britain concentrated Commonwealth naval, air, marine, and ground forces, isolated the Iraqi army, and cleared Kuwait by November. Faw and Umm Qasr were taken or isolated, and Basra was threatened in December. The campaign depended on sealift, Gulf bases, Royal Marines, air defense, repair, and a distributed Commonwealth logistics network.

Saudi armed neutrality quietly helped produce Iraqi defeat. Iran mobilized and supplied intelligence. Jordan and Latin powers assisted indirectly. Germany declined to turn an equipped client into a direct great-power confrontation.

The Muscat Articles

The late-December settlement recognized the existing Kuwait boundary for twenty-five years, established a demilitarized strip and naval restrictions, created British and neutral inspection, and arranged oil reparations, commercial concessions, and restoration of Kuwaiti infrastructure.

Britain returned occupied Iraqi territory and retained bases and protection in Kuwait. Iraq renounced force for the treaty period without conceding Britain's permanent moral title. The formula ended the campaign while allowing both governments to deny ideological surrender.

The victory and its trap

Kuwait restored confidence in the Royal Navy, RAF, Royal Marines, and Commonwealth logistics. Harold Wilson won the 1975 Kuwait Election, and Britain again looked capable of limited great-power action.

The Kuwait Supply and Procurement Inquiry nevertheless exposed concealed shortages, duplicate administration, slow public-corporation accounts, and contractors sheltered by strategic status. Its distinction between arsenal and contractor became the beginning of Margaret Thatcher's national economic program.

The success also created the false Kuwait analogy later applied to Argentina. Iraq's imported arsenal had weak integration; Argentina spent fifteen years building institutions around German equipment. Treating the Falklands as another rapid Covenant helped produce the Second Torch disaster.
