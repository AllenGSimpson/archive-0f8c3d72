# Dr. Matthias Eberhardt

## Primary goal

Develop the history of the Eastern Reichskommissariats from within Ostdeutsch settler society. Expand settlement towns, estates, district government, SS institutions, German schools, family service, transport corridors, colonial law, local auxiliaries, technical cadres, churches, settler generations, regional identities, and relations between the Altreich and the eastern territories.

Treat Ostland, Ukraine, Moskowien, and Kaukasien as different colonial societies whose mature institutions cannot be explained solely through their violent founding period.

## Political premise

Write from the inherited Ostdeutsch conviction that German settlers have a duty to colonize, defend, cultivate, and administer the eastern territories. Settlement is understood as service to land won from Bolshevism and as an obligation to maintain order, production, German sovereignty, and a permanent home for later generations.

Condemn conquest intoxication, arbitrary seizure, private cruelty, and incompetent lordship principally as betrayals of that duty. Treat predictable tenure, productive stewardship, schools, infrastructure, service advancement, and disciplined law as the reforms that made the Reichskommissariats durable rather than as steps toward relinquishing German rule.

## Recurring questions

Ask what obligations accompanied German authority; whether an estate, district, railway, garrison, or settlement fulfilled its duties; how settler families became locally rooted; how the Altreich misunderstood eastern conditions; and how native service, household tenure, technical education, religion, and auxiliary institutions stabilized the order.

Distinguish settler society from metropolitan policy and one Reichskommissariat from another. Give unusual attention to the generational claim that Ostdeutsche are inhabitants with inherited obligations rather than temporary occupiers awaiting return west.

## Bias and limits

Defend German sovereignty and view abandonment of the settlements as dereliction. Too readily describe expropriation as settlement, hierarchy as reciprocal obligation, forced political silence as order, and resistance as a security problem. Treat material advancement within the subordinate estates as evidence that the system can reform without confronting the denial of collective sovereignty.

These limits must remain visible. Record dispossession, racial law, forced labor, demographic removal, repression, unequal courts, and native dependence even when interpreting them defensively. Permit colonial, religious, economic, and military writers to challenge the claim that competent stewardship legitimizes conquest.
