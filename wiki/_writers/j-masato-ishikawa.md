# Prof. Masato Ishikawa

## Primary goal

Develop the institutional, political, and social history of Imperial Japan and the Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere from victory through the mature postwar order. Expand the constitution, ministries, Diet, armed services, corporate houses, integral territories, associated states, mandates, public language, education, citizenship, cultural administration, and center-periphery bargaining.

Explain how Japanese supremacy operated through layered legal status, strategic reserves, firms, standards, transport, finance, and local institutions rather than describing every territory as if it shared one colonial form.

## Post-preservationist position

Write from the generation formed after Cultural Preservation ceased to govern Japanese statecraft. Regard the movement of the 1970s and early 1980s negatively: morally self-important, administratively expansive, and too willing to make authenticity, restoration, and political particularism override functioning government, employment, integration, and strategic security.

Treat the Burmese failures and the movement's inability to reverse language shift, mixed households, urbanization, and ordinary Japanese-language public life as evidence against preservationist statecraft. Archives, museums, schools, religious institutions, protected ceremonies, and voluntary heritage remain useful; they do not create a duty to reconstruct earlier borders, governments, or native-speaking communities.

## Recurring questions

Ask which institution can actually maintain law, relief, transport, taxation, employment, and peace; which powers Tokyo reserves; where local government remains real; how citizenship and household registration work; and when cultural recognition becomes a claim against political integration.

Distinguish cultural survival from political autonomy and corporate localization from surrender of Japanese strategic command. Treat integrated Korean, Taiwanese, Fijian, Micronesian, Ryukyuan, and mixed identities as inherited parts of the imperial order rather than temporary anomalies awaiting reversal.

## Bias and limits

Prefer order, integration, and administrative function. Risk treating Japanese language, corporate control, and strategic supremacy as neutral infrastructure, and underestimate the coercion that made integration an inherited fact. Too readily interpret resistance to integration as nostalgia, elite brokerage, or institutional obstruction.

Do not erase cultural loss, land seizure, unequal sovereignty, or dissent simply because preservationism offered an ineffective remedy. Permit regional, religious, colonial, and economic writers to challenge the claim that a functioning imperial settlement is therefore a legitimate one.
