Second World War

The Second World War lasts from 1939 to the Lisbon settlement of 1947. Germany destroys the Soviet state, Japan secures the Southern Resource Area without fighting America, and Britain accepts a negotiated defeat after cumulative military, economic, and political exhaustion. No belligerent develops an atomic weapon in time to define the outcome.

The central divergence

The war begins recognizably in Europe, including Britain's victory in the First Battle of Britain. Its decisive divergence occurs in the Pacific: Japan pursues the Strike South strategy but deliberately excludes Pearl Harbor and American possessions from the target list. The United States therefore remains formally outside the general war and continues material aid without undergoing full mobilization.

This choice changes every later balance. Japan gains resources without awakening American industrial war; Germany faces no American army in Europe; Britain cannot assume eventual rescue; and no Manhattan Project produces a wartime bomb.

The war in the east

The German–Soviet War lasts from the historical opening of Operation Barbarossa in 1941 to the Ural settlement of 1946. Without an American army in Europe or a successful British continental return, Germany's 1945 offensives break the Soviet western strategic system. Moscow, Leningrad, and Stalingrad fall, are isolated, or cease to function as viable centers.

The Soviet collapse is political as well as military. An emergency conspiracy removes and executes Stalin; a collective survival leadership negotiates a frontier at approximately the Urals and secures the right to carry populations, factories, machinery, rolling stock, archives, stores, and military formations eastward.

The evacuation beyond the Urals denies Germany a functioning industrial heartland while leaving military districts, security organs, cadres, factories, and refugee administrations scattered across Siberia and Central Asia. Their fragmentation removes Britain's strongest remaining hope that the continental balance will reverse and creates the frontier later separating the German and Japanese systems.

The war in Asia

The Limited Southern War seizes British and Dutch possessions across Southeast Asia while refusing a wider perimeter toward Midway or the central Pacific. The Japanese Twenty-Fifth Army combines the 5th Division, 18th Division, and Imperial Guards Division against a multinational defense centered on the 11th Indian Division, 8th Australian Division, and later the Malay Regiment.

The Malayan campaign opens at Kota Bharu, breaks the northern line at Jitra, meets its strongest early defense at Kampar, and opens central Malaya at Slim River. Australian and Indian troops delay the final advance at Gemas and Muar; wounded prisoners left during the withdrawal are killed in the Parit Sulong massacre.

The Japanese landings on Singapore open bridgeheads at Sarimbun Beach and Kranji. The advance captures Bukit Timah, drives the last ridge defense through Pasir Panjang, and reaches the marked military hospital at Alexandra, where patients and staff are massacred. The fall of Singapore and East Indies campaign secure the western routes and resource area.

The Shōnan military administration turns conquest into government. The Sook Ching massacre, Changi prisoner system, requisition, rationing, and military currency make the south an occupation and logistics problem rather than an endless chain of island offensives.

Army resources return to China, where the 1943 Chongqing Operation and a stronger Ichi-Go campaign isolate organized resistance and allow Nanjing to claim reunification. Japan enters the settlement with Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Pacific possessions, and new strategic depth. It has won an empire without testing itself against the full American economy.

The Mediterranean and African failure

Britain attempts a Commonwealth-supported but primarily British Operation Torch against Algeria while Germany remains committed in the east. The initial landings succeed, but Axis reinforcement and air-submarine interdiction isolate the bridgehead. Harold Alexander surrenders rather than destroy the expedition in place.

The “African Dunkirk” or “Dunkirk Without the Boats” becomes Britain's Gallipoli: a military defeat, a political betrayal myth, and a warning that the empire cannot reproduce the rescue of 1940 at continental distance.

The defeat of Britain

American aid ends in early 1947 as “loans to a drowning man.” The decision removes food, fuel, aircraft spares, machine tools, merchant-shipping support, and confidence. During the Second Battle of Britain, German jets strike radar, operations rooms, communications, depots, fields, factories, and naval bases. British exploitation of German naval ciphers is exposed and closed with shore-based computing assistance.

The culminating operation is Unternehmen Friedensbrot, remembered as the White Parachutes. Long-range aircraft reach London with food, medicine, and leaflets rather than bombs. Crowds move from the drop zones toward Westminster. George VI refuses personal government and refuses the use of troops against peaceful demonstrators; Churchill's ministry loses confidence but remains long enough to negotiate.

The Lisbon settlement

Portugal mediates the 1947 conference. Germany wants a solvent British counterweight and an end to naval war. Italy wants a connected Mediterranean–Red Sea empire. Japan wants recognition of gains without assuming every British colonial burden. The United States is conspicuously absent.

Britain remains sovereign, parliamentary, and armed but loses Malta, Cyprus, Egypt, Suez control, Sudan, Malaya, Singapore, Hong Kong, Pacific possessions, and—through sale to Spain—Gibraltar. Germany receives continental predominance; Italy gains the Mediterranean hinge; Japan receives recognition of its Asian empire. The peace ends the war without settling which system represents the future.

A war without one accepted meaning

No defeated German or Japanese regime is replaced, no American liberation army supplies a common victor's narrative, and Britain remains sovereign after defeat. The result is not a universal postwar consensus but five rival traditions of Second World War memory.

Germany treats victory as proof of order; Japan as proof of strategic restraint and Asian responsibility; the Latin states as preservation of faith and hierarchy; Britain as honorable resistance joined to failed strategy; and the United States as a permanent argument over intervention. These accounts compete through schools, archives, film, games, tourism, and veterans' institutions into the twenty-first century.
