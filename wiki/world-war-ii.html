Second World War

The Second World War lasts from 1939 to the Lisbon settlement of 1947. Germany destroys the Soviet state, Japan secures the Southern Resource Area without fighting America, and Britain accepts a negotiated defeat after cumulative military, economic, and political exhaustion. No belligerent develops an atomic weapon in time to define the outcome.[a]

The central divergence

The war begins recognizably in Europe, including Britain's victory in the First Battle of Britain. Its decisive divergence occurs in the Pacific: Japan pursues the Strike South strategy but deliberately excludes Pearl Harbor and American possessions from the target list. The United States therefore remains formally outside the general war and continues material aid without undergoing full mobilization.

This choice changes every later balance. Japan gains resources without awakening American industrial war; Germany faces no American army in Europe; Britain cannot assume eventual rescue; and no Manhattan Project produces a wartime bomb.

The war in the east

The German–Soviet War lasts from the historical opening of Operation Barbarossa in 1941 to the Ural settlement of 1946. Without an American army in Europe or a successful British continental return, Germany's 1945 offensives break the Soviet western strategic system. Moscow, Leningrad, and Stalingrad fall, are isolated, or cease to function as viable centers.

The Soviet collapse is political as well as military. An emergency conspiracy removes and executes Stalin; a collective survival leadership negotiates a frontier at approximately the Urals and secures the right to carry populations, factories, machinery, rolling stock, archives, stores, and military formations eastward.

The evacuation beyond the Urals denies Germany a functioning industrial heartland while leaving military districts, security organs, cadres, factories, and refugee administrations scattered across Siberia and Central Asia. Their fragmentation removes Britain's strongest remaining hope that the continental balance will reverse and creates the frontier later separating the German and Japanese systems.

The Soviet fleets did not share one capitulation. The Baltic Fleet, employed as part of the Leningrad–Kronstadt fortress system, passed through partial scuttling and surrender after Leningrad's strategic defense ended on 14 December 1945; its surviving ships and facilities became a mixed German–Finnish inheritance. The Black Sea Fleet reached Poti and Batumi with no sea route to the eastern successor territories and scuttled most major hulls between 3 and 10 May 1946. The Pacific Fleet still possessed crews, docks, and a functioning naval hinterland when its 2 February 1947 standstill led to the Vladivostok Fleet Agreement on 19 February. Most of that fleet remained together under Japanese supervision and later supplied the Vladivostok and Far Eastern successor navies. Baltic infrastructure was inherited, Black Sea combat power was physically destroyed, and Pacific naval organization survived through negotiated subordination.[a]

The war in Asia

The Limited Southern War seizes British and Dutch possessions across Southeast Asia while refusing a wider perimeter toward Midway or the central Pacific. Britain's Singapore strategy fails at the opening: Robert Brooke-Popham's Far East Command does not launch Operation Matador in time, and Japanese naval aircraft destroy Tom Phillips's Force Z—Prince of Wales and Repulse—east of Kuantan. Tomoyuki Yamashita's Japanese Twenty-Fifth Army combines Takurō Matsui's 5th Division, Renya Mutaguchi's 18th Division, and Takuma Nishimura's Imperial Guards Division against Arthur Percival's multinational Malaya Command. Its principal field formations include Lewis Heath's III Indian Corps, David Murray-Lyon's 11th Indian Division, Gordon Bennett's 8th Australian Division, Merton Beckwith-Smith's late British 18th Division, and the Malay Regiment.

The Malayan campaign opens at Kota Bharu, breaks the northern line at Jitra, meets its strongest early defense at Kampar, and opens central Malaya at Slim River. Australian and Indian troops delay the final advance at Gemas and Muar; wounded prisoners left during the withdrawal are killed in the Parit Sulong massacre.

The Japanese landings on Singapore open bridgeheads at Sarimbun Beach and Kranji. The advance captures Bukit Timah, drives the last ridge defense through Pasir Panjang, where Adnan Saidi becomes the defining local defender, and reaches the marked military hospital at Alexandra, where patients and staff are massacred. The fall of Singapore captures the naval base; the East Indies campaign secures the western routes and resource area.

The Shōnan military administration turns conquest into government. The Sook Ching massacre, Changi prisoner system, requisition, rationing, and military currency make the south an occupation and logistics problem rather than an endless chain of island offensives.

Army resources return to China, where the 1943 Chongqing Operation and a stronger Ichi-Go campaign isolate organized resistance and allow Nanjing to claim reunification. Japan enters the settlement with Malaya, Singapore, the Dutch East Indies, Indochina, Pacific possessions, and new strategic depth. It has won an empire without testing itself against the full American economy.

The Mediterranean and Middle East campaign

Italy's war begins with overextension, divided service authority, and serious failures in Greece and North Africa. German assistance prevents collapse, but Rome retains supreme political and theater command. Under Ugo Cavallero, Comando Supremo gradually connects the army, battle fleet, air staff, merchant shipping, ports, and Rommel's German mobile group through an Italian Mediterranean command system.

Italian East Africa also survives the campaign that historically destroyed it. The survival of Amedeo's viceroyal government preserves African formations, ports, roads, airfields, records, and settler institutions long enough to turn wartime isolation into a permanent Red Sea position.

The First Battle of El Alamein remains an Axis defeat. The convoy system then accumulates the reserve for an Italian-led victory at the Second Battle of El Alamein. The breakthrough opens a prolonged advance across Egypt. Britain retains the Egyptian and Sinai front into its late-war endgame before the Suez campaign secures the canal and closes Britain's direct Mediterranean route to the Indian Ocean ahead of the 1947 settlement.[a]

Britain attempts a Commonwealth-supported but primarily British Operation Torch against Algeria while Germany remains committed in the east. The initial landings succeed, but the Axis counteroffensive combines Vichy, Italian, and German reinforcement with air-sea interdiction. Harold Alexander accepts the Algiers Capitulation rather than destroy the expedition in place.

The “African Dunkirk” or “Dunkirk Without the Boats” becomes Britain's Gallipoli: a military defeat, a political betrayal myth, and a warning that the empire cannot reproduce the rescue of 1940 at continental distance. For Italy, the wider Mediterranean and Middle East campaign becomes the institutional victory that underwrites its 1947 empire.

The defeat of Britain

American aid ends in early 1947 as “loans to a drowning man.” The decision removes food, fuel, aircraft spares, machine tools, merchant-shipping support, and confidence. During the Second Battle of Britain, German jets strike radar, operations rooms, communications, depots, fields, factories, and naval bases. British exploitation of German naval ciphers is exposed and closed with shore-based computing assistance.

The culminating event is the White Parachutes or Bread Raid of 28 May 1947. Me 364 crews and local ground personnel replace the intended final bomb load with food, medicine, and leaflets; Friedensbrot is the later German propaganda name, not an operational codename. Crowds move from the drop zones toward Westminster. George VI refuses personal government and refuses the use of troops against peaceful demonstrators. Churchill resigns on 2 June, Attlee's Emergency Ministry seeks terms on 3 June, and the preliminary ceasefire takes effect on 8 June.[a]

The Lisbon settlement

Portugal mediates the 1947 conference. Germany wants a solvent British counterweight and an end to naval war. Italy wants a connected Mediterranean–Red Sea empire. Japan wants recognition of gains without assuming every British colonial burden. The United States is conspicuously absent.

Britain remains sovereign, parliamentary, and armed but loses Malta, Cyprus, Egypt, Suez control, Sudan, Malaya, Singapore, Hong Kong, Pacific possessions, and—through sale to Spain—Gibraltar. Italy converts wartime possession of Libya, Tunisia, Algeria, and Saharan routes into a differentiated North African system rather than one uniform annexation. Germany receives continental predominance; Italy gains the Mediterranean hinge; Japan receives recognition of its Asian empire. The peace ends the war without settling which system represents the future.

A war without one accepted meaning

No defeated German or Japanese regime is replaced, no American liberation army supplies a common victor's narrative, and Britain remains sovereign after defeat. The result is not a universal postwar consensus but five rival traditions of Second World War memory.

Germany treats victory as proof of order; Japan as proof of strategic restraint and Asian responsibility; the Latin states as preservation of faith and hierarchy; Britain as honorable resistance joined to failed strategy; and the United States as a permanent argument over intervention. These accounts compete through schools, archives, film, games, tourism, and veterans' institutions into the twenty-first century.
