Falklands–Malvinas War

The Falklands–Malvinas War was a prolonged naval-air siege between Britain and a sovereign German-equipped Argentina. It began as a sovereignty seizure, became a test of Commonwealth endurance, and ended in a restrained British victory whose political boundaries were drawn by America.

The failed diplomatic road

The Lisbon talks of 1981–82 and the 1983 Brazilian–Vatican Rio formula failed. Islanders rejected any automatic road to Argentine rule; Buenos Aires rejected indefinite postponement. Argentina set 31 March 1984 as the deadline for a sovereignty-transfer framework.

This was no improvised diversion by a collapsing junta. Argentina had spent fifteen years integrating German doctrine, equipment, training, maintenance, submarines, missiles, armor, and domestic vehicle production into a national military project.

Operación Rosario

Argentina attacked in April 1984 without a traditional declaration of war. A reinforced British garrison of roughly 800–1,000 regulars and 200–300 local volunteers fought for about three days. Argentina then moved 12,000–16,000 personnel, artillery, mobile air defense, anti-ship missiles, radar, electronic warfare, and runway engineers onto East Falkland.

Britain won the South Georgia campaign and pushed the Argentine surface fleet toward mainland cover, but aircraft, missiles, and quiet U-boats inflicted severe escort and transport losses.

Second Torch

Margaret Thatcher initially applied lessons from Kuwait and authorized a July 1984 East Falkland landing. Integrated Argentine air, missile, mine, artillery, submarine, electronic, and ground defenses defeated it.

Thatcher accepted responsibility and adopted the No Third Torch doctrine: no operation merely because delay was politically uncomfortable. Britain established the West Falkland lodgment, developed infrastructure, blockaded East Falkland, and waited for a sustainable return.

Armed mediation and the long siege

Patrick Buchanan imposed South Atlantic armed mediation: no German troops or serving crews, no British mainland or regime-change war, American monitoring, and an eventual settlement based upon local self-government. Ronald Reagan restated those boundaries in the Five Falklands Rules.

The resulting South Atlantic siege joined blockade, aviation, missiles, submarines, covert resupply, construction, and repair. Germany's Operation Nachtbrücke and American surveillance made the limits an active contest.

The 1985 campaign

Winter Quay and Argentine Tridente contested the West Falkland base. Lantern degraded the East Falkland warning network, and Crown Gate established the San Carlos bridgehead. Blackthorn took Goose Green, but Cóndor del Sur nearly split the lodgment at Sussex Ridge. Sceptre then captured Mount Pleasant before the eventual advance on Stanley.

The corrected armored campaign pitted British Chieftain Mk 10s and supporting vehicles against Argentine TAM-74A2s, Panzer XI Ausf. D(ar)s, VCTP-74s, and VBE-78 Ñandús. Challenger, Warrior, and Panzer XII did not serve in the campaign.

The capture of Stanley

Repaired runways, land-based air cover, blockade, attrition, repair capacity, and methodical ground operations allowed Britain to take Stanley in 1986. The victory preserved the Commonwealth and validated strategic endurance while leaving the casualties, shipping failures, and political haste behind Second Torch in public memory.

Punta del Este Protocols

The American-, Brazilian-, Uruguayan-, and Vatican-mediated protocols restored British administration and required islander consent for any later sovereignty change. Argentina withdrew, renounced force for a fixed period, ended war-related German arrangements, and accepted limits on German southern bases.

Britain accepted no mainland occupation, regime change, or punitive unlimited exclusion zone. A multinational commission verified the settlement. Britain won the islands; the United States defined how far victory could extend.

The Falklands Commission condemned discounted warnings, Kuwait analogies, the haste behind Second Torch, and inadequate reserve shipping and lift while validating later siege planning and Factory Reownership capacity. Thatcher accepted the findings and retired voluntarily in late 1987; the resulting leadership contest and Peace Election placed reconstruction under David Ashcombe.
