Great-power crisis · 1967–1968

Iceland Crisis and Atlantic Accord

The Iceland Crisis brings German and American fleets to the edge of war and produces the first formal geographic restraint between their systems. Its settlement prevents immediate conflict by dividing the Atlantic in a way Britain regards as a second defeat.

German intervention

In 1967–68 Germany moves forces into Iceland after an ambiguous Icelandic appeal for assistance. Berlin describes the deployment as protection and stabilization inside the European security area. Washington treats it as a test of whether German state power can cross into the Atlantic and eventually threaten the Americas.

Iceland's location matters more than its size. Airfields, submarine approaches, weather, signals, and the Greenland–Iceland–Britain gap make control of the island a question about the boundary between continents and oceans.

The hidden naval imbalance

American and German surface forces confront one another in the North Atlantic. American planners rely heavily on battleship presence and visible fleet power. Unknown to Washington, modern German Type XX attack submarines are positioned to devastate the American force if firing begins.

Neither side wants a war whose escalation rules are undefined. Germany can inflict an immediate naval shock but cannot assume America will accept it. America can reinforce the Atlantic but cannot know the size, position, or acoustic character of the German submarine screen.

The Mid-Atlantic Ridge Accord

President Kennedy negotiates the 1968 Mid-Atlantic Ridge Accord, later commonly called the Atlantic Accord. Germany and the United States agree not to deploy state armed forces into the other's hemisphere. Iceland falls on the European and German side; Greenland and the Americas fall on the American side.

The agreement regulates state force rather than influence. Trade, firms, advisers, volunteers, intelligence, political money, and covert action remain loopholes. The accord therefore creates a restraint line, not an alliance or comprehensive peace.

The powers between the lines

Britain interprets the bargain as America consigning it to Germany's sphere and denying its status as an independent great power. London fortifies Canada, Bermuda, Jamaica, Belize, the Falklands, and other Atlantic positions and plans to survive without automatic American protection.

Canada is equally alarmed: geographic inclusion in the American hemisphere sounds less like protection than a possible claim of tutelage. Its planners prepare border delay, evacuation, Shield and Rockies defense, militias, stay-behind networks, Arctic logistics, and continued Commonwealth aid.

The accord succeeds narrowly. It prevents a German–American war, bounds later rivalry, and supplies the model eventually used at Tehran: recognition lines constrain great powers without necessarily erasing the sovereignty of smaller states within them.
