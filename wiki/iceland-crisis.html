Great-power crisis · 1967–1968

Iceland Crisis and Atlantic Accord

The Iceland Crisis brings German and American fleets to the edge of war and produces a formal geographic restraint between their systems.[a] Its settlement prevents immediate conflict by dividing the Atlantic in a way Britain treats as another loss of great-power standing.[e]

German intervention

In 1967–68 Germany moves forces into Iceland after an ambiguous Icelandic appeal for assistance.[a] Berlin describes the deployment as protection and stabilization inside the European security area. Washington treats it as a test of whether German state power can cross into the Atlantic and eventually threaten the Americas.[e]

Iceland's location matters more than its size. Airfields, submarine approaches, weather, signals, and the Greenland–Iceland–Britain gap make control of the island a question about the boundary between continents and oceans.[e]

The hidden naval imbalance

American and German surface forces confront one another in the North Atlantic. American planners rely heavily on battleship presence and visible fleet power. Unknown to Washington, modern German Type XXX nuclear Jagd-U-Boote are positioned to devastate the American force if firing begins.[a]

Neither side wants a war whose escalation rules are undefined. Germany can inflict an immediate naval shock but cannot assume America will accept it. America can reinforce the Atlantic but cannot know the size, position, or acoustic character of the German submarine screen.[e]

Operational balance

The confrontation brought unlike naval systems into the same operating area. The United States displayed a surface force whose battleships made resolve visible and could fight German surface ships. Germany used visible ships and troops within a wider system that included concealed nuclear attack submarines, shore analysis, patrol information, and prepared firing positions. The authorial reconstruction fixes a catastrophic American opening defeat if the crisis becomes a naval battle; it does not fix the precise order of fire, number of boats, ships lost, or duration of the engagement.[a]

American neutrality remained part of this balance without freezing the United States Navy in its 1947 condition. Two decades of postwar construction had produced modern ships, aircraft, sensors, and submarines. The institutional starting point was nevertheless an intact capital fleet that had not passed through a belligerent Atlantic escort expansion, sustained convoy campaign, or peer submarine war. The Philippine, Honduran, and Venezuelan experiences tested amphibious movement and regional intervention; they did not reproduce a contested North Atlantic against Germany's mature undersea arm.[e]

The crisis therefore exposed two different contingencies. The United States could lose the opening naval battle despite greater visible surface power. Germany could win that battle without obtaining a governable peace. Kennedy's settlement removed the immediate firing problem before either government had to discover how much escalation, replacement, commerce warfare, alliance movement, and political endurance followed the first salvo.[e]

The 1968 Atlantic settlement

President Kennedy negotiates the 1968 settlement, described in current records as either the Mid-Atlantic Ridge Accord or the Atlantic Accord; its exact formal title remains open.[e] Germany and the United States agree not to deploy state armed forces into the other's hemisphere. Iceland falls on the European and German side; Greenland and the Americas fall on the American side.[a]

The agreement regulates state force rather than influence. Trade, firms, advisers, volunteers, intelligence, political money, and covert action remain loopholes. The accord therefore creates a restraint line, not an alliance or comprehensive peace.[a]

The powers between the lines

Britain interprets the bargain as America consigning it to Germany's sphere and denying its status as an independent great power. London fortifies Canada, Bermuda, Jamaica, Belize, the Falklands, and other Atlantic positions and plans to survive without automatic American protection.[a]

Canada is equally alarmed: geographic inclusion in the American hemisphere sounds less like protection than a possible claim of tutelage.[e] Its planners prepare border delay, evacuation, Shield and Rockies defense, militias, stay-behind networks, Arctic logistics, and continued Commonwealth aid.[a]

The accord succeeds narrowly. It prevents a German–American war and bounds later rivalry.[e] The later Tehran settlement uses a comparable recognition-line structure, constraining great-power deployments without automatically erasing the sovereignty of states inside the resulting regions.[e]
