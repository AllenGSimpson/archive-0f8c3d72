# Naval Armaments Reference, 1944–1985

## Purpose and authority

This is the specialized canon reference for warships, naval auxiliaries, submarine systems, fleet doctrine, and naval development in the alternate-World-War-II setting. It establishes the 1947 armistice inventory, postwar class lineages, fixed 1985 force structure, and the naval balance immediately before the Falklands/Malvinas War.

Within that subject, this document supersedes:

- provisional aggregate Latin-bloc fleet totals that treated Italy as overwhelmingly dominant;
- any implication that all five King George V-class battleships survive the war;
- the earlier generic phrase “Type XX attack submarine” for Germany’s 1968 nuclear boats;
- approximate displacement, air-group, speed, endurance, and payload bands where this document supplies one reference value;
- descriptions of Argentina as incapable of building modern warships;
- descriptions of patrol vessels and littoral combatants as interchangeable categories;
- any strategically important ballistic-missile-submarine force dependent upon nuclear warheads;
- the assumption that transport submarines move merchant-ship quantities of ordinary cargo.

Later direct user corrections still take precedence. Historical ship names retained here are intentional where the hull, construction program, or naming tradition survives the divergence.

Individual hull records are maintained in [Naval Asset Registry, 1947–1985](./navalAssetRegistry%20-%20AltWWII.csv). The registry distinguishes explicit canon from historical carryovers and extrapolated hulls. This document controls class characteristics and force totals when a registry row is less authoritative.

## Scope

Included:

- surviving American, British, German, Italian, and Japanese combatants at the 1947 armistice;
- postwar development through 31 December 1985;
- Britain and the Commonwealth, Germany, Japan, the Philippines, the United States, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Occitania, and Argentina;
- battleships, carriers, cruisers, destroyers, frigates, corvettes, missile craft, submarines, amphibious ships, mine-warfare vessels, and major fleet auxiliaries;
- German and Japanese submersible transports;
- the forces controlling the opening naval balance of the Falklands/Malvinas War.

Not covered exhaustively:

- harbor craft, Coast Guards, fisheries services, river forces, and civilian merchant fleets;
- every landing craft carried aboard a larger amphibious ship;
- minor boats below 250 tonnes unless they carry strategic missiles or perform mine warfare;
- complete weapons-magazine layouts for every refit;
- exact squadron assignment on every date;
- final Falklands battle losses, which remain to be written campaign by campaign.

## Naming, measurement, and status rules

1. Displacement is full-load surface displacement unless `submerged` is explicitly stated.
2. Introduction means commissioning of the first operational hull, not launch or trials.
3. A ship may be physically afloat without being operational. `Operational` means capable of active service within ninety days in 1947 and within six months in the 1985 fleet tables.
4. `Front-line` means suitable for major combat rather than training, colonial duty, or immediate disposal.
5. Britain, America, Japan, Italy, Spain, Portugal, Occitania, and Argentina name commissioned major warships. Germany normally names carriers and cruiser-sized surface combatants but identifies destroyers, frigates, fast craft, and submarines chiefly by hull number.
6. Japanese submarines use `I-` for large ocean boats and `Ro-` for smaller boats. German submarines use `U-` numbers.
7. Commission and decommission fields in the asset registry may contain a year when the exact day is not canon. `date_precision` identifies that limitation.
8. A blank decommission field means active, reserve, or otherwise extant on 31 December 1985 unless the disposition field states otherwise.
9. Shipboard gun calibers are fixed values. Missiles are counted by ready cells or launch positions where known.
10. Aircraft counts describe the standard 1985 wartime air group, not maximum deck parking.

## Registry reconstruction standard

The armistice totals are canon; the survival of most individual wartime hulls is not recoverable after the divergence. The asset registry therefore uses a class-allocation reconstruction rather than anonymous balance rows:

1. Every pre-1948 record has a service-style hull number or pennant, designation, class, subtype, propulsion plant, full-load displacement, standard crew, armament or payload, and principal role.
2. Submarines record both surface full-load and submerged displacement. Carriers additionally record a standard air group.
3. A historically named survivor retains its historical class and hull or pennant number where one was used. Italian, Spanish, Portuguese, Occitanian, and Argentine warships always carry personal names; their hull numbers remain administrative identifiers rather than substitutes for names.
4. Class design and reference specifications follow ships built, laid down, or in service by 1945 whenever possible. Paper designs appear only where the victorious extended war supplies a specific reason for completion, such as German M-class cruisers, Type 1944 torpedo boats, Type XX transports, Italian *Comandanti Medaglie d'Oro*, and Japanese improved *Agano* cruisers entering service in 1946–1947.
5. Commission years, survival to 31 December 1947, operational condition, postwar retirement, transfer, and conversion remain alternate-history allocations. These are identified as `Extrapolated hull` or `Working extrapolation`, not falsely presented as real-world ship histories.

The United States allocation preserves the setting's limited mobilization: its 145 destroyers include twenty-five surviving flush-deckers and substantial Farragut-through-Sims inheritance, while exactly seventy-two Fletcher, Allen M. Sumner, and Gearing hulls represent the new wartime program. Germany's 820 surviving submarines are distributed across Types II D, VII C, VII C/41, IX C/40, IX D2, X B, XIV, XVII B, XX, XXI, and XXIII instead of appearing as an undifferentiated residual block.

American armistice-era cruisers use their historical `CB`, `CA`, and `CL` service numbers and class-roster names. Where an OTL cruiser name depended upon commemorating a wartime loss, the no-loss setting chronology retains the vessel's original planned name or another historically allocated name from the same class, preventing simultaneous duplicate names. Named Imperial Japanese surface ships use their historical names and, where the wartime source records one, their construction or program number; numeric kaibōkan and submarines retain their historical `No.`, `I-`, or `Ro-` identities rather than synthetic `C-`, `D-`, or `CD-` registry placeholders.

Spanish commissioned ships use the prefix `BAE` (*Buque de la Armada Española*), replacing the provisional `SNS`. The postwar Spanish naming system makes destroyers city ships, frigates commemorations of national and naval heroes, amphibious ships provinces or historic regions, and minesweepers and fleet auxiliaries saints or Marian titles. Corvettes draw from capes and winds, while submarines use marine animals, inventors, and foundational martial names. Prewar ships retain their historical names even where those names predate the later system.

The other named Latin and South Atlantic navies remain deliberately distinguishable. Italy retains its historical named class rosters and uses virtues, admirals, explorers, regions, islands, rivers, scientists, and coastal cities for postwar construction. Portugal uses cities for destroyers, explorers and commanders for frigates, islands for corvettes, regional names for amphibious ships, saints for mine warfare, and rivers for auxiliaries. Occitania favors Occitan city forms, troubadours and regional figures, winds, regions, saints, rivers, and scientists. Argentina retains its established hero, province, geographic, and martial naming traditions, with named mine-warfare ships rather than anonymous `MCM` entries.

Spain, Portugal, Occitania, and Argentina are not part of the locked five-navy armistice ledger. Their registry starting forces are working reconstructions based on historically existing local or inherited classes. Portugal therefore uses its five *Vouga*-class destroyers and three *Delfim*-class submarines rather than preserving earlier arbitrary totals of six and four.

## United States inheritance

America never enters the general war and suffers no combat attrition. Its twenty-five battleships consist of fifteen older standard and pre-standard types, two North Carolina-class, four South Dakota-class, and four Iowa-class ships. Montana and Ohio remain under construction and commission in 1948 and 1950.

America completes thirteen fleet carriers by 1947 rather than the historical mass carrier program. It builds seventy-two new destroyers for American or export use; fifty older destroyers and thirty-six modern ships pass to Britain or Commonwealth customers. The American merchant emergency program produces 720 EC2/Liberty-type ships, 180 faster Victory-type ships, and 250 tankers or specialized cargo hulls rather than 2,710 Liberty ships.

## British inheritance

Britain ends the extended war with eleven capital ships afloat:

- Hood;
- Renown;
- Nelson;
- Rodney;
- Queen Elizabeth;
- Valiant;
- King George V;
- Duke of York;
- Anson;
- Howe;
- Vanguard.

Hood survives because the historical Denmark Strait battle never occurs, but structural exhaustion forces retirement in 1950. Prince of Wales and Repulse remain Far Eastern losses. Britain loses 175 destroyers and 95 submarines during the war but completes and receives enough replacements to retain 168 destroyers and 260 major escorts afloat.

## German inheritance

The five German capital ships are Bismarck, Tirpitz, Scharnhorst, Gneisenau, and Friedrich der Große, the first H-39 completing trials. Großdeutschland commissions in 1949. Graf Zeppelin and Peter Strasser provide the two-carrier force.

Germany commissions 1,300 U-boats through 1947 and retains 820 physically extant hulls. Only 310 are front-line:

| Family | Front-line hulls |
|---|---:|
| Type XXI | 110 |
| Type XXIII and related coastal Elektroboote | 40 |
| Modernized Type VII and IX | 100 |
| Minelaying, transport, reconnaissance, and special boats | 60 |

The remaining 510 boats are training, second-line, repairable reserve, obsolete, damaged, or awaiting disposal. This vast inventory is rapidly reduced but supplies crews, machinery, test hulls, export boats, and the industrial base for the later Type XXIV–XXXV line.

## Italian inheritance

Italy retains Littorio, Vittorio Veneto, Roma, Impero, Andrea Doria, and Caio Duilio. Five are operational. Aquila and Sparviero are completed carriers. Italy avoids the political seizure, partition, and treaty restrictions that historically destroyed fleet continuity.

## Japanese inheritance

Japan retains ten battleships: Yamato, Musashi, Nagato, Kongō, Haruna, Kirishima, Ise, Hyūga, Fusō, and Yamashiro. Mutsu is lost in its magazine accident, and Hiei is lost during extended Indian Ocean combat.

Japan retains fifteen carriers, thirty-seven cruisers, 126 destroyers, 105 major escorts, and ninety-six submarines. It fights Britain and regional enemies but never suffers American carrier war, submarine blockade, industrial replacement pressure, or mining of the home islands.

# V. Britain and the Commonwealth

## Strategic character

The Royal Navy is the armed infrastructure of the Siege Commonwealth. Its priorities are sea-lane survival, anti-submarine warfare, expeditionary logistics, carrier air cover, defense of Canada and Atlantic possessions, and enough heavy surface power that Germany cannot dismiss Britain as a defeated island.

## Naming and pennant doctrine

Royal Navy vessels carry personal names in the traditional form **HMS Name**. Pennants such as K19, F101, D12, or S07 are administrative hull identifiers and are never substituted for the ship's name in the designation field. Wartime survivors retain the historical naming families of their classes: flowers for Flower-class corvettes, rivers for River-class frigates, castles for Castle-class escorts, hunts for Hunt-class ships, tribal names for Tribal-class destroyers, and the established alphabetic group names for fleet destroyers and submarines. Alternate completions extend the same period naming rule rather than receiving generic numbered names.

Historical names may be reused after the earlier bearer leaves commission, but not while both ships are simultaneously in service. Because the armistice fleet suffers fewer losses and some wartime hulls serve longer than in real history, several postwar historical names are replaced by unused equivalents from the same naming family. Royal Fleet Auxiliary vessels use the prefix **RFA**, while commissioned warships use **HMS**.

# VI. Germany

## Strategic character

> The surface fleet fixes, threatens, and displays. The U-boat fleet destroys.

Germany maintains enough surface power to cover U-boat exits, protect Norway and Iceland, escort replenishment groups, support client fleets, and demonstrate technology. It does not attempt to match Japanese or American carrier numbers.

# VII-A. Philippine Independence Fleet

## Strategic character

The Independence Fleet is the first visibly sovereign armed institution of the postwar Philippine state. Japan transfers the ships outright rather than leasing them or retaining a right of reversion. Japanese dockyards refit the destroyers and escorts before the June 1960 transfer; the elderly cruiser Tama is transferred after a more limited command-and-training refit. Japanese advisers remain important during the fleet's first years, but the vessels commission under Philippine names, Philippine hull numbers, and Philippine command.

The fleet is not designed to fight the Imperial Japanese Navy. It provides coastal patrol, inter-island movement, disaster relief, training, anti-smuggling work, and a core around which an independent navy can develop. Captain Isidro Cabahug is the principal Philippine operational advocate for a balanced transfer containing a flagship, combatants, and economical escorts rather than a purely ceremonial cruiser.

# IX. The Latin Naval System

## Common institutions

The Latin Naval Materiel Board, established in 1958, standardizes fuel connections, ammunition interfaces, replenishment practice, helicopter-deck markings, tactical data formats, missile interfaces, 533 mm torpedo tubes, rescue equipment, and radio frequencies without imposing a single hull design.

By the 1970s the bloc shares or interoperates with Occitan anti-ship missiles, Italian Teseo heavy missiles, medium-range SAMs, 30×170 mm close-in cannon, 533×6,100 mm torpedoes, a Latin tactical data link, and modular VLS cells.

## Industrial position

Argentina is an arsenal-state fleet, not a helpless importer. Río Santiago, Tandanor, and Puerto Belgrano can build or assemble corvettes, patrol ships, landing ships, auxiliaries, missile craft, hull sections, and eventually complete licensed submarines. # XI. Operation Nachtbrücke and the Falklands balance

## German undersea bridge

Operation Nachtbrücke assigns one Type XXXI/T and one Type XXXV to covert supply through Puerto Belgrano and East Falkland. Argentina calls its element Puente Sumergido.

| Ship type | Successful deliveries | Delivered per voyage | Total delivered |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Type XXXI/T | 3 | 650 t | 1,950 t |
| Type XXXV | 2 | 950 t | 1,900 t |
| Total | 5 | — | 3,850 t |

The cargo consists principally of missiles, artillery and tank ammunition, radar and sonar components, engines, medical supplies, specialized lubricants, communications equipment, and technicians. It prolongs Argentine resistance but cannot sustain the whole garrison.

The Type XXXV normally crosses 6,700 nmi from northern Europe to Puerto Belgrano and then operates the 800 nmi Falklands route. A Falklands shuttle cycle takes eleven days. A winter-night unloading evolution transfers 400 tonnes in five hours; the full load requires two nights away from a developed berth.

## British South Atlantic force

The principal formation on 1 January 1985 includes:

- Commonwealth and New Zealand, fleet carriers;
- Invincible, light carrier;
- Lion, missile battleship;
- Devonshire and Kent, missile cruisers.

Standard simultaneous escort strength is eleven destroyers, eighteen frigates, six SSNs, four SSKs, and seven mine-warfare ships. The first-year theater pool is sixteen destroyers, twenty-six frigates, eight SSNs, six SSKs, and ten mine-warfare ships.

The amphibious and support pool includes three landing-platform docks, two helicopter assault ships, six landing ships, eight troop or vehicle transports, six oilers, seven stores ships, three repair ships, and three casualty ships, plus chartered merchants.

Britain is nationally stronger, but Argentina can concentrate near mainland air cover, repair yards, coastal missiles, mines, short-range submarines, and short logistical routes. The decisive naval questions are detection, convoy survival, carrier protection, ASW helicopter availability, repair capacity, and American limits on attacks against the Argentine mainland.
