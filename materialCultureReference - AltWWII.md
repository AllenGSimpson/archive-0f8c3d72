# Material Culture and Civilian Industry Reference

## Status and authority

- **Scope:** civilian industry, firms and brands, household technology, transportation, retail, technical labor, commercial standards, design, and everyday material life from the 1947 armistice through 1985.
- **Canonical source:** the timestamp-ordered **Worldbuilding Minor Details** branch archived in [transcript.md](./transcript.md), chiefly Turns 412–579.
- **Related references:** the [Setting Bible](./settingBible%20-%20AltWWII.md), [Setting Timeline](./settingTimeline%20-%20AltWWII.md), [Greater East Asian Co-Prosperity Sphere Reference](./coProsperitySphereReference%20-%20AltWWII.md), and the dedicated [land](./landArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md), [aerospace](./aerospaceArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md), [naval](./navalArmamentsReference%20-%20AltWWII.md), and eventual computing references.
- **Authority rule:** direct user corrections and specialized references control. Developments carried forward by later prompts are working canon. Illustrative firm names, unsettled marques, and exact product designations remain proposals until explicitly confirmed.
- **Boundary:** this document describes the civilian and dual-use consequences of technology. It does not override military equipment specifications, the established strategic computing and space chronologies, or the Co-Prosperity Reference's rules for Japanese corporate ownership, associated-state sovereignty, transport concessions, currency, and semiconductor geography.

## Governing summary

The setting does not leap from wartime scarcity to a sleek alternate future. Its material culture develops through three overlapping transformations:

1. **Conversion and improvisation, 1947–1955:** surviving industrial combines, local workshops, surplus vehicles, reconstruction agencies, and regional dealers keep society moving.
2. **Applied systems, 1955–1970:** reliability engineering, transistors, automatic control, refrigeration, nuclear engineering, modern logistics, jet travel, and the space race begin changing ordinary products and services.
3. **Platforms and networks, 1970–1985:** compact electronics, machine-readable commerce, household video, personal computing, dedicated automotive electronics, and incompatible bloc networks turn products into connected systems.

Development is uneven. Metropolitan citizens see new appliances, radios, cars, calculators, video, and computers while colonial and peripheral populations may encounter the same technical order as rail control, registration systems, plantation refrigeration, surveillance, or a repaired wartime truck. Competence and prosperity never imply moral legitimacy.

## The industrial inheritance of 1947

### Conversion without dismantling

No major bloc has completed civilian conversion by the end of 1947. Unlike the historical postwar order, victorious German and Japanese firms are not dismantled by occupation. Major combines and aircraft concerns survive with their personnel, supplier relationships, patents, machine tools, and political connections substantially intact.

Important surviving structures include:

- Germany's IG Farben, Reichswerke, automobile groups, electrical combines, optics houses, and aerospace firms;
- Japan's zaibatsu, Nakajima industrial capacity, electrical firms, shipyards, optics makers, and precision workshops;
- Italy's IRI system and surviving automobile, aviation, shipbuilding, office-machine, and energy concerns;
- Britain's distributed state, private, and Commonwealth reconstruction industries;
- America's automobile, appliance, electrical, office-machine, and telecommunications firms, still shaped by prolonged Depression conditions rather than total-war mobilization.

Cross-bloc ownership becomes a durable problem. Opel, Ford-Werke, Dehomag, British and Dutch properties in Japanese Asia, and colonial concessions may remain legally foreign-owned while operating under local controls, blocked dividends, compulsory reinvestment, or politically constrained boards.

### The small-firm layer

The great combines do not eliminate local economic life. In 1947–1955, ordinary people depend upon:

- repair garages and village mechanics;
- dealers who also extend credit and locate spare parts;
- remanufacturers of engines, tires, electrical motors, pumps, and machine tools;
- regional bus and haulage companies;
- local builders and prefabrication shops;
- farm-equipment workshops;
- refrigeration, cold-storage, and food-processing firms;
- radio, telephone, and office-equipment repairers.

Many future conglomerates begin in precisely these unglamorous fields. Honda begins with powered bicycles and small engines; Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo with small electronics and recording equipment; Zuse's protected office becomes Zuse KG in 1948; Finmeccanica is prepared in late 1947 and formally organized in 1948.

### The first postwar consumer symbols

Each bloc's early emblem joins a product to a political promise:

- **Germany:** the people's car and restored continental road system;
- **Italy:** the scooter, compact car, and designed household object;
- **Japan:** the powered bicycle, compact radio, camera, and practical machine;
- **Britain:** reconstructed housing, merchant shipping, electrical service, and the Land-Rover;
- **America:** the automobile, refrigerator, television, and household appliance.

These are not merely objects. Each requires electricity, credit, roads or broadcast infrastructure, repair service, component standards, and a functioning distribution network.

## Surplus motorization, 1947–1955

Germany and Britain release large stocks of military soft-skinned vehicles into agriculture, reconstruction, municipal work, approved private haulage, and colonial service. Japan releases a smaller proportion at home because more of its motor pool remains useful across the maritime empire. The eastern Reichskommissariats rely heavily upon captured Soviet vehicles and machinery as well as German surplus.

The most useful civilian inheritance is not tanks. It is:

- trucks and utility cars;
- motorcycles and sidecars;
- ambulances;
- trailers and mobile workshops;
- generators, compressors, cranes, and fuel bowsers;
- tractors and engineering equipment;
- spare engines and drivetrain components.

Tracked and armored vehicles survive mainly as recovery machines, forestry tractors, construction equipment, colonial-security assets, or scrap. Fuel, tires, batteries, and parts—not the number of surviving chassis—set the practical ceiling.

The surplus phase has a recognizable rhythm:

| Period | Character |
|---|---|
| 1947–1949 | Release, salvage, improvised conversion, mixed military and civilian paint |
| 1950–1952 | Maximum civilian utility and rapid growth of repair networks |
| 1953–1956 | Replacement crisis as worn fleets become uneconomic |
| After 1955 | New standardized vehicles dominate metropolitan purchases; surplus persists in colonies, farms, and remote districts |

Its defining visual is a civilian lorry whose new paint has worn through to a wartime marking beneath.

## The 1950 equilibrium

### Germany

Germany reaches stabilized austerity rather than abundance. Volkswagen civilian delivery is controlled and outstanding KdF claims are resolved through purchase certificates rather than free cars. Auto Union remains intact in Saxony with DKW, Wanderer, Audi, and Horch. Opel and Ford-Werke continue as American-owned but politically blocked German enterprises.

The motor market divides among motorcycles and sidecars, Volkswagen and small DKW family vehicles, Opel/Ford/Borgward commercial and middle-class cars, and Mercedes-Benz/Horch/BMW prestige vehicles. Shops, dealers, municipal fleets, and firm vehicles matter more than a fully private mass-car society.

### Japan

Japan's imperial economy is strong in shipbuilding, heavy industry, precision production, and the use of subsidiaries throughout Asia, but private motorization remains limited. Nakajima is reorganized into a broad and difficult industrial concern. Honda, Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, and similar firms demonstrate that the postwar order can still create new enterprises beneath the zaibatsu.

### Britain and the Commonwealth

Britain's material culture remains tied to reconstruction and dispersed imperial logistics. Standard merchant designs, electrical repair, public housing, utility vehicles, and Commonwealth production are more representative than metropolitan luxury. The Land-Rover becomes both a domestic and imperial machine.

### United States

America retains a wider independent automobile field. Nash-Kelvinator is the strongest surviving independent; Kaiser-Frazer is weaker; Tucker becomes a cult failure rather than a major manufacturer. The Big Three remain dominant without yet erasing American Motors-type, Studebaker-Packard, and Kaiser-Willys paths.

### Latin bloc

SEAT's 1950 foundation anchors Spanish motorization. Italy combines Fiat, scooters, IRI infrastructure, Olivetti offices, and energy development. Portugal emphasizes ports, merchant repair, power, and colonial connections. Occitania's importance lies increasingly in aerospace, controls, electronics integration, and high-value prototypes.

## 1952–1955: the postwar order becomes ordinary

By the middle of the decade, emergency arrangements harden into institutions.

- German metropolitan consumers see growing production without unrestricted choice; eastern settlers receive standardized services while subject communities experience extraction, registration, and unequal infrastructure.
- Mittelafrika's visible modernity clusters around mines, railways, ports, plantations, administrative towns, refrigerated storage, and settler enclaves.
- Japan standardizes imperial procurement and expands motorcycles, three-wheel delivery vehicles, radios, cameras, port equipment, and coastal shipping.
- The Philippine war economy increases demand for light transport, field refrigeration, portable radios, water purification, prefabrication, tropical medicine, and maintenance.
- Britain replaces improvised wartime shipping and equipment through Commonwealth standard families and regional variants.
- America finally achieves durable recovery, with automobiles, television, appliances, suburban building, airlines, and defense procurement expanding together.

The crucial social figure is the repairman who can keep incompatible generations of equipment working. The prestige engineer has not yet displaced the practical mechanic.

## 1955–1960: the applied-systems takeoff

### The Raumbote transition

Before 1957, rocketry, radar, reactors, automatic control, jet propulsion, materials science, and electronic calculation remain separate expert worlds. **Raumbote I** reveals that Germany can coordinate them into one national system. The satellite therefore changes procurement as much as popular imagination.

After 1957, governments buy not only rockets but tracking radar, teleprinters, gyroscopes, vacuum chambers, pressure vessels, instrumentation, weather stations, communications relays, photographic film, reliability laboratories, technical-school places, and superior machine tools.

This is why the space competition survives the Moon landing. Weather, navigation, communications, surveillance, stations, launch cadence, rescue, and industrial reliability are continuing services rather than single victories.

### Reliability engineering

Aircraft, reactors, submarines, automatic factories, and launch vehicles teach governments and firms that complex systems fail through ordinary components. Permanent quality and reliability groups develop around:

- supplier inspection and component traceability;
- failure analysis and statistical control;
- environmental, vibration, pressure-cycle, and fatigue testing;
- serialized parts and maintenance schedules;
- formal accident inquiry and certification.

Civilian appliances and vehicles benefit through better bearings, insulation, seals, heat treatment, wiring, and records even when they contain no literal spacecraft component.

### Transistors and automatic control

Transistors first matter through lower power use, shock resistance, and portable batteries. They spread through radios, hearing aids, test sets, field telephones, aircraft electronics, vehicle radios, medical monitors, and compact controllers.

Automation does not yet mean an empty factory. It means transfer lines, relay sequencing, punched-tape machine tools, automatic gauges, centralized pump control, telephone exchanges, and classification-yard switching. Skilled labor shifts toward maintenance electricians, instrument technicians, tool setters, inspectors, control-room operators, and production planners.

### Materials, nuclear engineering, and refrigeration

Jet, reactor, and rocket programs improve alloys, synthetic rubber, high-temperature steels, seals, lubricants, fiberglass, adhesives, plastics, ceramics, insulation, paints, and coatings. Marketing terms such as “rocket tested,” “orbital precision,” and “space sealed” quickly outrun the underlying engineering.

Early nuclear spillovers include radiation instruments, medical isotopes, industrial radiography, pumps, valves, shielding, remote handling, sterilization research, metallurgy, and high-reliability controls. Britain sells nuclear development as **atoms against blockade**; Germany as continental modernity; Japan as industrial self-sufficiency.

Refrigerated warehouses, trucks, ships, pharmaceuticals, milk systems, and household refrigerators reshape food supply and imperial geography. The refrigerator becomes a political symbol of power generation, chemistry, distribution, credit, and maintenance working together.

### Logistics and standards

Pallets, forklifts, standardized crates, and early freight containers reduce handling and pilferage. Each bloc initially develops incompatible boxes, crane fittings, railway dimensions, and documentation. A plug, radio frequency, container fitting, film size, tape format, or vehicle part can bind a smaller state to a bloc more effectively than a speech.

Technical assistance increasingly decides whose measurements, electrical systems, rail equipment, communications protocols, spare parts, and maintenance schools will prevail. Imperial influence is expressed through the parts catalogue.

## Bloc character at the 1960 baseline

### Germany: coordinated continental systems

Volkswagen becomes common without becoming universal. Motorways acquire standardized service stations, repair depots, touring guides, roadside restaurants, resort parking, and breakdown telephones. Telefunken carries national technical authority; Grundig serves the mass radio and television market; Braun develops a modern domestic design language; Blaupunkt expands vehicle radio.

The rocket supply chain encompasses Zeiss optics, Telefunken telemetry, Siemens power equipment, Bosch controls, IG Farben chemicals, pumps, bearings, seals, connectors, film, and heavy transport. Eastern territories receive the industrial control layer—rail signals, substations, pipeline control, telephones, machinery stations, German labels—without equal household prosperity.

### Japan: compact useful machines

Honda defines small-engine and motorcycle mobility; Toyota, Nissan, Isuzu, and Hino emphasize trucks, buses, taxis, and institutional fleets; three-wheel delivery vehicles remain common. Nikon, Canon, Minolta, Seiko, Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo, Matsushita, and Sharp build strength in optics, radios, recording, timing, and compact products.

Yaskawa, Omron, Hitachi, Toshiba, NEC, and specialist suppliers expand motor control, relay logic, automatic inspection, industrial timers, punched-tape control, and transfer machinery. Japanese shipyards and ports pursue tankers, ore carriers, refrigerated ships, diesel engines, cargo documentation, radar, weather routing, and early containers.

### Britain: practical reliability at distance

Commonwealth standard ship families, regional spare-part compatibility, Land-Rovers, nuclear power, certification, weather, signals, and mechanized ports answer Britain's distance and blockade problems. Comet structural failures create stronger fatigue testing, full-scale rigs, manufacturer records, and independent inquiry. The long-run export is not only an aircraft but confidence in British certification.

### America: private abundance and federal research

The Philippine War and Raumbote shock create a federal defense-research and procurement state even while parties deny they are restoring the New Deal. Highway, suburban, airline, television, appliance, model-rocket, hi-fi, and science-kit cultures grow together. America answers Germany's launcher culture with the pilot-centered image of the reusable winged spaceplane.

### Latin bloc: integrated design at moderate scale

Fiat, Vespa, Lambretta, Olivetti, AGIP, and IRI give Italy a recognizable blend of mobility, office administration, energy, infrastructure, and design. SEAT and Pegaso anchor Spanish motorization and commercial transport. Occitania supplies guidance, aerodynamics, controls, and prototypes. The bloc's early space ancestry lies in tracking, telemetry, upper-atmosphere research, Mediterranean communications, and African site studies rather than an immediate crewed race.

### Working 1960 baseline

- The metropolitan surplus economy is largely ending.
- Only America approaches mass dependence upon private automobiles.
- Television is a major political medium; radio remains more geographically universal.
- Portable transistor electronics are a normal product category.
- Commercial jets are prestigious rather than ordinary mass travel.
- Automatic control and numerical machinery are entering leading factories without eliminating labor.
- Palletization and incompatible container systems are spreading.
- Nuclear technology remains mostly institutional, with visible medical and industrial spillovers.
- Space has become a permanent public, industrial, and educational competition.

## 1960–1965: instrumented modernity

Consumer computing remains indirect. Ordinary people encounter reservations, bills, payroll, banking records, scheduling, and inventory produced by computers rather than owning computers themselves. The cathode-ray tube is universal; flat displays remain experimental; light-emitting diodes are small indicators.

Monochrome television remains dominant while color television begins a bloc standards contest. The transistor radio is the defining personal electronic product. The Olivetti Programma 101 and comparable small institutional machines make computation visible in offices and laboratories without creating a home-computer market.

Automotive competition explores diesel, turbocharging, rotary engines, gas turbines, fuel injection, radial tires, disc brakes, improved suspension, seat belts, and crash deformation. The NSU rotary becomes a larger international story but continues to face sealing, economy, and durability problems. Turbine cars remain demonstrations. Analog gauges and mechanical systems still define ordinary cars.

Environmental policy speaks chiefly in terms of smoke, poison, dirty water, occupational health, urban sanitation, conservation, and efficiency. Carbon emissions do not organize politics, and nuclear power enjoys stronger legitimacy than in the historical timeline.

The aesthetic field remains plural:

- neoclassicism survives as a living language of state power;
- technical modernism represents competence and systems;
- restorative historicism communicates continuity;
- commercial neo-futurism sells plastics, appliances, automobiles, airlines, and space;
- Italian Futurism persists longer as a design and propaganda vocabulary.

Ordinary homes remain conventionally arranged. The space age appears through shaped appliances, plastics, television, switches, fins, dashboards, packaging, and advertising rather than through universally futuristic architecture.

## Japanese brands and the Asia-first market

Japanese firms do not automatically adopt English-facing identities. Their usual hierarchy is:

1. a legal corporate name;
2. an imperial consumer mark;
3. a local sales identity or product description.

Roman letters are common because they travel across scripts, but this does not make the name semantically English. China requires companion character marks and formal “character clearance”; Korea and Formosa receive more directly Japanese identities; Southeast Asia and the Philippines tend to receive romanized Japanese marks with local-language explanations.

Controlling working treatments include:

- Tokyo Tsushin Kogyo does **not** become Sony in 1958; its durable consumer identity is **Totsuko**, possibly accompanied by a character mark.
- Matsushita and **National** remain more prominent than a historical-timetable Panasonic identity.
- Honda remains Honda, but Taiwan and Southeast Asia replace California as formative overseas markets.
- Sharp and Canon remain viable international marks.
- Nissan is the parent group and **Datsun** the mass-market export marque.
- Toyota retains Crown and Century; there is no Lexus during the reference period, while Prince may remain a prestige and performance competitor.

Japanese products signify Japanese modernity throughout Asia rather than disguising themselves as American objects.

## Motorsport as a civilian laboratory

### Formula One

Formula One survives as the international name. National competition revives in 1948–1950, a provisional cup is organized in 1951, and the first alternate world championship begins in **1952**. German and Italian institutions supply the foundation; a neutral Geneva office administers the series. Drivers' and manufacturers' championships exist from the beginning.

The foundational field includes Mercedes-Benz, Auto Union, Alfa Romeo, Ferrari, and Maserati. British constructors later disrupt the heavy continental teams through lightweight chassis and independent engineering. America remains strongest in oval, endurance, and Group 7 competition; Japan enters through motorcycles, touring cars, and engines before becoming a Formula One power.

Formula One rules govern energy, mass, and safety while leaving engineering paths comparatively open. Regulations are stable across multiple seasons and emergency intervention requires demonstrated danger rather than political preference. International arbitration reduces bloc litigation.

### Group 7

A near-unrestricted **Group 7** experimental category emerges around 1964–1966. It becomes the home of six wheels, all-wheel drive, turbines, fan downforce, large wings, ground effect, movable aerodynamics, active suspension, composites, and other concepts too disruptive or specialized for the main championship.

### Technology transfer

Racing feeds production through fuel injection, brakes, tires, alloys and composites, suspension, limited-slip differentials, aerodynamics, turbo durability, bearings, lubricants, crash structures, restraints, data acquisition, semi-automatic controls, and engine management. Endurance, rally, and touring competition often transfer road technology more directly than Formula One; Formula One remains the supreme prestige laboratory.

## 1965–1970: systems society

By 1970 the leading firms occupy recognizable niches.

### Germany

- Volkswagen is forced to diversify beyond one people's car.
- Auto Union remains a multi-marque technical group associated with front-wheel drive and experimental engines.
- Mercedes-Benz owns luxury, diesel durability, safety, and long-distance prestige.
- BMW combines sporting sedans, motorcycles, and premium mobility.
- Porsche joins sports cars, racing, and contract engineering.
- NSU carries rotary and compact experimentation.
- Borgward survives in reorganized form, strongest in commercial vehicles and weaker or absorbed in passenger cars.
- Siemens and AEG span power, industry, household appliances, and automation.
- Telefunken remains the prestige broadcast and defense-electronics name; Grundig mass radio/television; Braun design-led premium goods; Philips a Dutch-German bridge and probable cassette originator.

### Japan

- Mitsubishi, Mitsui, and Sumitomo remain imperial systems houses.
- Nakajima is a large, politically protected, technically capable but difficult aviation-machinery concern.
- Toyota, Nissan–Datsun, Prince, Honda, and Toyo Kogyo occupy mass, truck, prestige, small-engine, and rotary niches.
- Totsuko, Matsushita/National, Sharp, Casio, Seiko, Omron, and Yaskawa link portable electronics, appliances, calculators, timing, and factory control.

### Britain

British Motor group, Leyland, Rover/Land-Rover, Jaguar, Lotus, Cooper, Ferranti, GEC, English Electric, Pye, and EMI preserve a broad but sometimes duplicated industrial field. The country's comparative advantage increasingly lies in high-value machinery, engines, aviation, signals, computing, certification, and technical design rather than mass low-value production.

### America

The Big Three dominate automobiles without completely eliminating American Motors, Kaiser-Willys, or a Studebaker-Packard successor. IBM, GE, Westinghouse, RCA, Motorola, Zenith, Xerox, Polaroid, Corning, Fairchild, and Texas Instruments-type firms anchor office systems, consumer electronics, materials, imaging, and integrated circuits.

A prosperous 1970 household may own a color or high-quality monochrome television, cassette recorder, hi-fi, washer, refrigerator-freezer, several appliances, a telephone, a car, and personal transistor radios. It does not normally own a personal computer, flat television, home video recorder, digital watch, electronic dashboard, or networked appliance.

## 1970–1975: the microelectronic household

By 1975:

- color television is established but not universal;
- compact cassette is the leading portable recording medium in Europe and Asia, with greater format competition in America;
- video recording is normal in institutions and rare in homes;
- pocket calculators are real, while scientific and programmable models remain expensive;
- quartz timekeeping is spreading and digital display remains conspicuous;
- auto-exposure cameras are mainstream and autofocus experimental;
- arcade machines and dedicated television games precede a mass personal-computer market;
- hobby computers appear as kits in 1975;
- barcodes and rival machine-readable product systems enter large warehouses and retailers;
- bank cards, terminals, and automated services spread while cash and checks remain dominant;
- satellites improve broadcasting, weather, and long-distance communication without producing personal mobile service.

Appliances are “automatic” principally through electromechanical controls and narrow electronics. Medicine gains electronic monitoring, laboratory automation, and advanced imaging before homes gain general computing.

Without the historical oil shock, American large cars survive longer. Fuel economy still matters through trade, military experience, urban congestion, competition, and engineering. Rotary, diesel, turbocharging, injection, radial tires, disc brakes, and safety improve visibly; turbine cars remain marginal. Dedicated car electronics handle specific functions rather than controlling the entire vehicle.

The era's visual future moves from fins toward dark display windows, glowing numerals, modular buttons, and control panels. Germany's consumer-space emblem is the Raumhafen as a service system; Japan's is portable precision; America's is the approaching private programmable machine; Britain's is the high-value Commonwealth technical economy.

## 1975–1980: the programmable consumer world

Personal computers become real household and small-business products, though adoption remains uneven.

- **America** leads open documentation, replaceable architecture, independent software, spreadsheets, word processing, hobbyist clubs, retail shops, and games.
- **Japan** emphasizes polished hardware, sound, display, games, compactness, and native non-Latin use.
- **Germany** creates a substantial but regulated offline home market under Kurzplan 77.
- **Britain** links educational machines, public terminals, videotex, and translation services.
- **The Latin bloc** presents the computer as a designed professional and household instrument.

Cassette and disk distribution make software control imperfect. Arcade, console, and computer games become related but distinct businesses. The German falling-block game **Mauerwerk** appears around 1979 and immediately develops conflicting creator, employer, state-distributor, and foreign-license claims.

Home video becomes established but not yet universal. Japanese firms dominate format competition. Portable headphone cassette listening becomes a defining Japanese export. Quartz and digital watches become ordinary. Industrial robots remain specialized and require programmers, maintenance, tooling, inspection, and predictable workpieces.

Cars acquire electronic ignition, limited diagnostic systems, premium anti-lock braking, and electronic fuel injection without becoming computers on wheels. Digital dashboards and navigation remain prototypes or high-cost demonstrations.

## 1980–1985: platform society

### Computing and telecommunications

By 1985, consumers and institutions choose platforms rather than isolated machines. Compatibility, software, support, language handling, peripherals, training, and politics determine purchases.

Five digital systems coexist: the American Data Network, Germany's Weltnetz, Japanese imperial networks, Commonwealth packet and videotex systems, and Latin public/professional systems. UNICODE 80 supports multilingual identity but does not erase incompatible files, operating systems, protocols, or commercial standards.

Fax is unusually important in the Japanese sphere because it moves characters, handwriting, diagrams, and seals without requiring every terminal to share the same text system. Cellular service exists, but car telephones and pagers are more common than pocket handsets.

### Media and games

Home video and rental normalize. A longer-duration licensed Japanese format is the probable mass-market winner, while Totsuko retains a premium or professional position; final format names remain open. Camcorders are expensive but real. Optical digital audio is a prestige cross-bloc product and does not immediately replace records or cassettes.

Game consoles become platform businesses with publisher licensing and quality control. America experiences a market correction rather than an industry-wide extinction because computers, arcades, and multiple regional platforms remain viable.

### Offices, retail, and records

Electronic typewriters, word processors, fax machines, photocopiers, and computers increase paper use rather than abolish it. Machine-readable commerce expands through bank, travel, credit, warehouse, employment, and retail records. ATMs and cards are familiar in prosperous urban regions without eliminating cash.

### Transportation and industry

Automobiles are electronically managed but mechanically legible. Premium cars may combine electronic injection, diagnostics, anti-lock braking, climate control, and digital instruments. Ordinary repair still requires mechanical judgment.

Formula One and Group 7 become data-driven laboratories. Industrial robotics remains concentrated in repeatable welding, painting, handling, inspection, and machine tending. Space programs improve remote operation, medical monitoring, communications, water processing, materials, and reliability more than they create recognizable “space gadgets.”

### Détente and protected commerce

Commercial détente makes foreign goods more visible by 1985, but quotas, ownership rules, local assembly, state purchasing, incompatible standards, and bloc finance prevent historical-style global convergence or rapid industrial hollowing. Japanese goods retain Asia-first names. German products emphasize certification and service. American products emphasize ecosystem and user freedom. British goods emphasize reliability and interfaces. Latin products emphasize design and intelligibility.

The 1984–1985 Falklands/Malvinas War is the first major conflict experienced through home video, satellite news, commercial networks, and private digital analysis. Civilian information systems reveal their strategic value without becoming one universal network.

## What an affluent household owns in 1985

Common or increasingly common:

- cathode-ray-tube television and video recorder;
- stereo, radio, records, and cassettes;
- quartz watches and pocket calculators;
- refrigerator-freezer, automatic washer, and other mature appliances;
- telephone and perhaps an answering or recording device;
- personal computer in an affluent, technical, educational, or small-business household;
- bank cards and access to automated cash services;
- a mechanically conventional car with electronic ignition and perhaps premium electronic controls.

Not yet ordinary:

- flat-panel television;
- pocket cellular telephone;
- digital still camera;
- laptop computer;
- mass online shopping;
- consumer satellite navigation;
- household robot;
- mass-market electric car;
- universal Internet access;
- always-connected appliances;
- artificial-intelligence assistant.

## Social geography and lived texture

### The technician class

The period creates a new middle-class technical worker between craftsman and university engineer: the certified maintenance technician. He or she services radar, refrigeration, automatic machinery, medical electronics, telephone exchanges, aircraft instruments, broadcast equipment, and later computers. Manuals, tools, clearances, and portable credentials provide unusual occupational mobility.

### Metropolitan and colonial inequality

The same bloc can be simultaneously modern and archaic. A German settler community may watch television while a neighboring subject village relies upon a repaired Soviet tractor. A Japanese port may operate electronic freight control while rural associated territories use hand records. A British Commonwealth laboratory may lead global research while a distant colony depends upon an aging standard truck.

The frontier of modernity is therefore not a national border. It follows ports, railways, power grids, laboratories, military bases, hospitals, schools, favored districts, and supply chains.

### The ordinary look of the future

The future arrives as layers rather than replacement. A 1985 room may contain traditional furniture, a large CRT, a VCR, a plastic telephone, a personal computer, paper files, and religious or dynastic imagery. A street combines interwar buildings, neoclassical public monuments, technical-modern additions, neon, scooters, buses, and increasingly electronic shop signs.

This is not a clean retrofuture. Old institutions and visible history remain present inside newer systems.

## Canon boundaries and open design work

The following are intentionally unsettled and should be resolved in [Questions and Confirmations](./questionsAndConfirmations%20-%20AltWWII.md) or later specialist work:

- exact ownership and survival path of disputed automobile and electrical firms;
- the industrial geography of the two French successor states;
- final names and standards for bloc container systems, color television, video, barcode, and early cellular networks;
- the exact 1952 Formula One calendar, teams, drivers, engines, and scoring;
- legal names, companion character marks, and export identities for several Japanese firms;
- final corporate structures and product names for fictional post-1947 firms;
- precise consumer adoption rates by country, region, class, race, and colonial status;
- the technical architecture of the five digital systems, which belongs in a future Computing Reference.
